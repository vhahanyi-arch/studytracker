# Unit 16 — Interpreting and discussing results

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 8, objectives 8Ss.04 and 8Ss.05. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### Averages and range

- **Mean:** add all the values and divide by how many there are.
- **Median:** the middle value when the data is in order (for an even number of values, the mean of the middle two).
- **Mode:** the most common value (there may be more than one, or none).
- **Range:** largest value − smallest value. It measures spread (how consistent the data is), not size.

The mean uses every value but is pulled by an extreme value (an outlier). The median is not affected by outliers. The mode is the only average for categorical data.

### Comparing two distributions — 8Ss.04

To compare two sets of data, compare **one average** (which set is bigger or better on the whole) and **the range** (which set is more consistent). Write each comparison in context, as a full sentence, using the values.

For data in a frequency table, mean = sum of (value × frequency) ÷ total frequency.

### Interpreting data and drawing conclusions — 8Ss.05

- Say what the data shows, and also what it does not show.
- Consider variation: a small difference between two samples may just be chance.
- Consider the sample: was it large enough, and representative?
- Look for misleading features in graphs: axes not starting at zero, uneven scales, missing labels.
- Data can support a conclusion without proving it.

## Key facts and formulas

| Formula | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Mean = total ÷ number of values | The "fair share" average | Numerical data without big outliers. |
| Median = middle value, position (n + 1)/2 | The middle of ordered data | Data with outliers. |
| Mode = most common value | The most typical | Categorical data, or most popular choice. |
| Range = largest − smallest | Spread | Judging consistency. |
| Mean from a table = Σ(value × frequency) ÷ Σfrequency | Σ means "add up all" | Frequency tables. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: comparing two classes

**Example.** Class A's test marks have mean 62 and range 30. Class B's have mean 58 and range 12. On average Class A did better (higher mean). Class B's marks were more consistent (smaller range).

### Situation: averages from a frequency table

**Example — goals per match.** Goals: 0 (3 matches), 1 (5), 2 (7), 3 (4), 4 (1). Total matches = 20.

- Mean = (0 × 3 + 1 × 5 + 2 × 7 + 3 × 4 + 4 × 1) ÷ 20 = 35 ÷ 20 = 1.75 goals.
- Mode = 2 goals (frequency 7).
- Median: the 10th and 11th values. Running totals 3, 8, 15, so both are 2: median = 2 goals.
- Range = 4 − 0 = 4 goals.

### Situation: choosing the best average

**Example — an outlier.** Weekly pay at a small firm: $300, $320, $310, $330, $1 500. The mean is $552, which is higher than four of the five wages. The median, $320, represents a typical worker better.

### Situation: drawing a conclusion

**Example.** In a survey of 20 students, 11 prefer online lessons. Saying "most students in the country prefer online lessons" is not justified: the sample is small, and from one school. A fair conclusion: "In this sample, slightly more than half preferred online lessons; a larger, wider sample is needed."

### Situation: spotting a misleading graph

**Example.** A bar chart of sales of $102 000 and $108 000 starts its vertical axis at $100 000, so the second bar looks four times as tall. The real increase is only about 6%.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing only the averages and forgetting the range (or the other way round).
- Saying "the range is higher, so they did better": range measures spread, not size.
- Finding the median without putting the data in order first.
- In a frequency table, dividing by the number of rows instead of the total frequency.
- Making conclusions bigger than the data supports.
