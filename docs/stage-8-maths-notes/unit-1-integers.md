# Unit 1 — Integers

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 8, objectives 8Ni.01 to 8Ni.07. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### Order of operations — 8Ni.01

Work in this order: **B**rackets, then **I**ndices (powers and roots), then **D**ivision and **M**ultiplication from left to right, then **A**ddition and **S**ubtraction from left to right (BIDMAS). A square root sign or a fraction line acts like a bracket: work out everything under it or above it first.

### Multiplying and dividing integers — 8Ni.02

An integer is a whole number: positive, negative or zero. When you multiply or divide two integers:

- same signs give a positive answer: (−6) × (−4) = 24;
- different signs give a negative answer: (−36) ÷ 9 = −4.

With several numbers, count the negative signs: an even number of negatives gives a positive answer, an odd number gives a negative answer. Estimate first by rounding each number, so you can spot a wrong sign or a wrong size.

### Factors, multiples and primes — 8Ni.03

A factor divides exactly into a number; a multiple is in its times table. A prime has exactly two factors, 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, …); 1 is not prime. Every whole number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes, using a factor tree or repeated division.

- **HCF** (highest common factor): multiply the prime factors the numbers share, using the lower power of each.
- **LCM** (lowest common multiple): multiply every prime factor that appears, using the higher power of each.

### Types of number — 8Ni.04

The sets of numbers fit inside each other:

- natural numbers: the counting numbers 1, 2, 3, … (some books include 0);
- integers: the natural numbers, zero and the negative whole numbers … −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 …;
- rational numbers: any number that can be written as a fraction of two integers, such as 3/4, −5 (= −5/1), 0.75 (= 3/4) and 0.333... (= 1/3).

Every natural number is an integer, and every integer is a rational number, but not the other way round.

### Indices — 8Ni.05

In 5³, 5 is the base and 3 is the index (power): 5³ = 5 × 5 × 5. Two index laws for the same base:

- multiplying: add the indices, so 5³ × 5⁴ = 5⁷;
- dividing: subtract the indices, so 7⁶ ÷ 7² = 7⁴.

Any number (except 0) to the power 0 equals 1, because, for example, 7⁶ ÷ 7⁶ = 7⁰ and also equals 1.

### Squares and square roots — 8Ni.06

Squaring a negative number gives a positive answer: (−7)² = 49. So every positive number has two square roots: the square roots of 49 are 7 and −7 (written ±7). The √ sign on its own means the positive root: √49 = 7. Take care with a calculator: −7² means −(7²) = −49, but (−7)² = 49.

### Cubes and cube roots — 8Ni.07

Cubing keeps the sign: 3³ = 27 and (−3)³ = −27. So each number has exactly one cube root: ∛64 = 4 and ∛(−64) = −4. Learn the cubes 1, 8, 27, 64, 125 and the squares up to 15² = 225.

## Key facts and formulas

| Fact or formula | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| BIDMAS | Brackets, indices, division and multiplication, addition and subtraction | Any calculation with more than one operation. |
| + × + = +, − × − = +, + × − = − | Same signs positive, different signs negative (the same for ÷) | Multiplying or dividing negative numbers. |
| aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ | Add the indices | Multiplying powers of the same base. |
| aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ | Subtract the indices | Dividing powers of the same base. |
| a⁰ = 1 | Any non-zero number to the power 0 | Simplifying, or when the indices cancel. |
| HCF: shared primes, lower powers; LCM: all primes, higher powers | From the prime factorisations | Sharing into equal groups (HCF); things happening together again (LCM). |
| HCF × LCM = the two numbers multiplied | For two numbers | Checking an HCF or LCM answer. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: a calculation with several operations

**Example — powers first.** 3 + 4 × 2² = 3 + 4 × 4 = 3 + 16 = 19. With a bracket: (3 + 4) × 2² = 7 × 4 = 28.

**Example — left to right.** 20 − 12 ÷ 4 + 1 = 20 − 3 + 1 = 18. (Not 20 − 3 − 1 or 20 − 12 ÷ 5.)

**Example — a root as a bracket.** √(9 + 16) × 2 = √25 × 2 = 5 × 2 = 10. Note √9 + √16 = 3 + 4 = 7, which is different.

### Situation: negative numbers

**Example — products.** (−2) × (−5) × (−3): three negatives, so negative; 2 × 5 × 3 = 30, so the answer is −30.

**Example — division.** (−84) ÷ (−7) = 12 (same signs).

**Example — estimate.** −48 × 21 ≈ −50 × 20 = −1000. The exact answer is −1008, which agrees in sign and size.

### Situation: prime factors, HCF and LCM

**Example — prime factorisation.** 84 = 2 × 42 = 2 × 2 × 21 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7 = 2² × 3 × 7. And 90 = 2 × 3² × 5.

- HCF(84, 90): shared primes 2 and 3, lower powers: 2 × 3 = 6.
- LCM(84, 90): all primes, higher powers: 2² × 3² × 5 × 7 = 1260.
- Check: 6 × 1260 = 7560 = 84 × 90.

**Example — a word problem (LCM).** One bus leaves every 12 minutes and another every 18 minutes. They leave together at 9:00. LCM(12, 18) = 36, so they next leave together at 9:36.

**Example — a word problem (HCF).** 48 pencils and 60 pens are shared into identical packs with nothing left over. The greatest number of packs is HCF(48, 60) = 12, each with 4 pencils and 5 pens.

### Situation: index laws

**Example — multiply and divide.** 5³ × 5⁴ = 5⁷. 2⁹ ÷ 2⁴ = 2⁵ = 32. (3⁴ × 3²) ÷ 3⁶ = 3⁶ ÷ 3⁶ = 3⁰ = 1.

**Example — find the missing index.** 4ˣ × 4³ = 4¹⁰ means x + 3 = 10, so x = 7.

### Situation: squares, cubes and their roots

**Example — two square roots.** x² = 81 has two solutions: x = 9 or x = −9.

**Example — cube root of a negative.** ∛(−125) = −5, because (−5) × (−5) × (−5) = −125.

**Example — mixed.** (−4)² + (−2)³ = 16 + (−8) = 8.

### Situation: classifying numbers

**Example.** For −3, 0.6, 7 and 2/9: natural numbers: 7; integers: −3 and 7; rational numbers: all four (0.6 = 3/5).

## Common mistakes

- Working left to right without BIDMAS: 3 + 4 × 2 is 11, not 14.
- Forgetting that (−3)² = 9 but (−3)³ = −27.
- Giving only one square root when asked for all solutions of x² = 49.
- Multiplying the indices instead of adding them: 2³ × 2⁴ = 2⁷, not 2¹².
- Calling 1 a prime number.
- Mixing up HCF (a factor, no bigger than the smallest number) and LCM (a multiple, at least as big as the largest number).
