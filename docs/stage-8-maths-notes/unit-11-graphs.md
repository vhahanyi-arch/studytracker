# Unit 11 — Graphs

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 8, objectives 8As.04, 8As.05, 8As.06 and 8As.07. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### Linear functions from words — 8As.04

Many real situations have a fixed part plus an amount for each unit: "a taxi charges $3 plus $2 per km". Write this as y = mx + c, where c is the fixed part and m is the rate: y = 2x + 3 (x in km, y in dollars). Read it backwards too: y = 5x + 10 could mean "$10 to join, then $5 per visit".

### Tables of values and graphs — 8As.05

To draw the graph of a linear function:

- choose at least three x-values (including 0) and work out y for each;
- plot the points (x, y) and join them with a ruled straight line across the whole grid.

A third point is a check: if the points do not lie on one line, there is a mistake.

### Gradient and y-intercept — 8As.06

In y = mx + c:

- m is the **gradient**, the steepness: how much y increases when x increases by 1. Positive m slopes up to the right, negative m slopes down, and a larger number means steeper.
- c is the **y-intercept**: where the line crosses the y-axis (when x = 0).

Lines with the same gradient are parallel. The gradient between two points is the change in y divided by the change in x.

### Graphs in parts and intersections — 8As.07

A real-life graph can be made of several straight pieces, for example a journey: travel, stop (a flat part), travel again. Each piece has its own gradient (on a distance–time graph, the gradient is the speed).

Where two graphs cross, both are true at once: the x and y values at the intersection satisfy both relationships. In a comparison of two price plans, it is where they cost the same.

## Key facts and formulas

| Formula | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| y = mx + c | Straight line: gradient m, y-intercept c | Any linear function. |
| Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x | Rise over run | From two points or a graph. |
| Same gradient means parallel | Equal steepness | Comparing lines. |
| y-intercept: put x = 0 | Where the line meets the y-axis | Reading c, or plotting a first point. |
| Intersection: both equations true | The point where graphs meet | Comparing two plans or journeys. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: writing a function from words

**Example — taxi.** $3 plus $2 per km: y = 2x + 3. A 7 km ride costs 2 × 7 + 3 = $17.

**Example — reading backwards.** A plumber charges according to y = 40x + 25 (x hours, y dollars). This means a $25 call-out fee plus $40 per hour.

### Situation: table of values and plotting

**Example.** For y = 2x − 1 with x from −1 to 3: x = −1, 0, 1, 2, 3 gives y = −3, −1, 1, 3, 5. Plot (−1, −3), (0, −1), (1, 1), (2, 3), (3, 5) and join them with a straight line.

### Situation: reading gradient and intercept

**Example.** y = −2x + 5 has gradient −2 (down 2 for each 1 across) and crosses the y-axis at (0, 5).

**Example — rearrange first.** y = 7 + 3x is the same as y = 3x + 7: gradient 3, intercept 7.

**Example — parallel lines.** y = 4x − 1 and y = 4x + 6 are parallel (both gradient 4). y = −4x + 6 is not.

### Situation: equation from two points

**Example.** A line passes through (2, 7) and (5, 16).

- Gradient = (16 − 7) ÷ (5 − 2) = 9 ÷ 3 = 3.
- Using (2, 7): 7 = 3 × 2 + c, so c = 1.
- The equation is y = 3x + 1. Check with (5, 16): 3 × 5 + 1 = 16.

### Situation: a graph in parts

**Example — a journey.** A cyclist rides 12 km in 40 minutes, rests for 20 minutes, then rides 8 km more in 30 minutes. The distance–time graph has three pieces: a slope of 12 km per 40 min (18 km/h), a flat piece (speed 0), and a slope of 8 km per 30 min (16 km/h). The first part is steepest, so it is fastest.

### Situation: intersection of two graphs

**Example — two phone plans.** Plan A: $20 a month plus $1 per GB, y = x + 20. Plan B: $5 per GB, y = 5x. They meet where x + 20 = 5x, so x = 5 and y = 25. For 5 GB both cost $25; for less data B is cheaper, for more A is cheaper.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the gradient of y = 7 + 3x as 7. The gradient is the number multiplied by x.
- Plotting points but joining them with a curve or not extending the line.
- Calculating the gradient as change in x ÷ change in y (it is y over x).
- Forgetting the sign: a line sloping down from left to right has a negative gradient.
- Reading an intersection from a graph without saying what it means in the context.
