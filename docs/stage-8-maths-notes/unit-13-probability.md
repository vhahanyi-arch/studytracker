# Unit 13 — Probability

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 8, objectives 8Sp.01, 8Sp.02, 8Sp.03 and 8Sp.04. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### Probability scale

Probability measures how likely an event is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Write probabilities as fractions, decimals or percentages, never as "1 in 4" in an answer box.

### Complementary events — 8Sp.01

An event either happens or does not happen. These two are complementary, and their probabilities add up to 1:

P(not A) = 1 − P(A).

If the probability of rain is 0.35, the probability of no rain is 0.65.

### Listing combined outcomes — 8Sp.02

When two things happen together (rolling two dice, spinning a spinner and tossing a coin), list every possible outcome systematically:

- a list, for a small number of outcomes (HH, HT, TH, TT);
- a sample-space table (grid), for two events with several outcomes each;
- a tree diagram, showing each stage as branches.

The number of outcomes is the number for the first event times the number for the second.

### Theoretical probability — 8Sp.03

When all outcomes are equally likely:

P(event) = number of outcomes in the event ÷ total number of outcomes.

Count from the full list of combined outcomes, not from the list of different totals (some totals happen in more ways than others).

### Experiments and relative frequency — 8Sp.04

When you cannot work out a probability from equally likely outcomes, estimate it with an experiment:

relative frequency = number of times the event happened ÷ number of trials.

The more trials, the closer the relative frequency usually gets to the theoretical probability. A big difference after many trials suggests the object may be biased (unfair).

## Key facts and formulas

| Formula | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| P(not A) = 1 − P(A) | Complementary events add to 1 | "Not" questions. |
| P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes | Equally likely outcomes | Dice, coins, cards, fair spinners. |
| Total outcomes = outcomes of first × outcomes of second | Counting combined outcomes | Checking a sample space is complete. |
| Relative frequency = successes ÷ trials | Experimental probability | Estimating from data or experiments. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: complementary events

**Example.** P(a bus is late) = 0.15, so P(the bus is not late) = 1 − 0.15 = 0.85.

**Example — fractions.** A bag has red, blue and green counters. P(red) = 2/5. P(not red) = 3/5.

### Situation: two dice

**Example — sum of 7.** A sample-space table for two dice has 6 × 6 = 36 outcomes. A sum of 7 comes from (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1): 6 outcomes. P(sum of 7) = 6/36 = 1/6.

**Example — a double.** Doubles: (1,1) to (6,6), 6 outcomes. P(double) = 6/36 = 1/6, and P(not a double) = 5/6.

**Example — sum of 2.** Only (1,1): P = 1/36. So 7 is far more likely than 2, even though both are possible totals.

### Situation: a coin and a spinner

**Example.** A coin is tossed and a spinner numbered 1 to 4 is spun. Outcomes: H1, H2, H3, H4, T1, T2, T3, T4 (2 × 4 = 8). P(heads and an even number) = 2/8 = 1/4.

### Situation: comparing experiment and theory

**Example — a coin.** A coin is tossed 50 times and lands heads 31 times. Relative frequency = 31/50 = 0.62, compared with the theoretical 0.5. With only 50 tosses this could be chance; toss it many more times before deciding it is biased.

**Example — a dice.** A dice is rolled 600 times and lands on 6 a total of 160 times. Relative frequency = 160/600 = 0.27, compared with 1/6 = 0.17. With 600 rolls this large difference suggests the dice is biased.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a probability greater than 1 or less than 0.
- Missing outcomes when listing: always use a systematic order or a table.
- Treating the 11 totals of two dice (2 to 12) as equally likely.
- Expecting experimental results to match theory exactly after a few trials.
