# Unit 6 — Collecting data

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 8, objectives 8Ss.01, 8Ss.02 and 8Ss.03. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### Choosing what data to collect — 8Ss.01

Start from the question you want to answer, then decide what data would answer it and how to collect it (survey, experiment, measurement, or existing records).

Types of data:

- **categorical:** names or labels, such as favourite sport or eye colour;
- **discrete:** numbers that can only take separate values, usually by counting, such as the number of siblings or goals;
- **continuous:** measurements that can take any value in a range, such as height, mass or time.

**Primary data** is collected by you; **secondary data** was collected by someone else (books, websites, records).

### Sampling — 8Ss.02

The **population** is everyone or everything you want to know about. A **sample** is the part you actually collect data from. Sampling saves time and money, but the sample must represent the population.

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole population (census) | Ask everyone | Complete and accurate | Slow and expensive for large groups |
| Random sample | Every member has an equal chance (names drawn from a hat, random numbers) | Avoids bias from choosing | Needs a full list; may by chance miss a group |
| Systematic sample | Every kth member from a list (every 10th name) | Quick and spread out | Can be biased if the list has a pattern |
| Convenience sample | Whoever is easiest to ask (friends, the first 20 people) | Very quick | Usually not representative, so biased |

A larger sample usually gives more reliable results, but takes longer to collect.

### Recording and representing data — 8Ss.03

Record data with a tally chart or a frequency table. Group continuous data into class intervals that do not overlap, such as 150 ≤ h < 160. A two-way table records two categories at once.

Choose a representation that suits the data:

- bar chart or vertical line chart: categorical or discrete data;
- pie chart: how a whole is split into parts;
- frequency diagram (bars touching): grouped continuous data;
- line graph: data that changes over time;
- scatter graph: two numerical variables for the same items.

### Good questionnaires — 8Ss.01

- Ask clear, short questions with one meaning.
- Give response boxes that do not overlap and cover every answer ("0 to 4", "5 to 9", "10 or more").
- Avoid leading questions ("Don't you agree that…?").
- Avoid personal questions that people may not answer honestly.

## Key facts and formulas

| Fact or formula | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Pie chart angle = (frequency ÷ total) × 360° | Each item's share of the circle | Drawing a pie chart. |
| Frequency = angle ÷ 360° × total | The reverse | Reading a pie chart. |
| Class intervals such as 10 ≤ t < 20 | Includes 10, not 20 | Grouping continuous data without overlap. |
| Larger random sample means more reliable | Less effect of chance | Judging a sampling plan. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: identifying the type of data

**Example.** Number of pets: discrete. Time to run 100 m: continuous. Favourite fruit: categorical. Shoe size: discrete (only certain sizes exist), even though feet lengths are continuous.

### Situation: judging a sampling method

**Example — biased sample.** To find out how often students in a school use the library, Sam asks 30 students inside the library. This is biased: students in the library are likely to use it more than others. Better: a random sample of 30 from the whole school register.

**Example — systematic sample.** A school has 600 students on an alphabetical list. To choose 50, take every 600 ÷ 50 = 12th name, starting at a random number from 1 to 12.

### Situation: designing a question

**Example — fix the boxes.** "How many hours of TV do you watch each week? 0–5, 5–10, 10–15" is poor: someone who watches 5 hours fits two boxes and 20 hours fits none. Better: "0 to 4", "5 to 9", "10 to 14", "15 or more".

### Situation: drawing a pie chart

**Example.** 60 students chose a favourite sport: football 24, basketball 15, tennis 12, swimming 9.

- Each student is worth 360° ÷ 60 = 6°.
- Football 24 × 6° = 144°; basketball 90°; tennis 72°; swimming 54°.
- Check: 144 + 90 + 72 + 54 = 360°.

### Situation: grouping continuous data

**Example.** Heights (cm): 152, 168, 147, 159, 171, 163, 155, 160. With classes 140 ≤ h < 150, 150 ≤ h < 160, 160 ≤ h < 170, 170 ≤ h < 180 the frequencies are 1, 3, 3 and 1. The value 160 goes in 160 ≤ h < 170, not in 150 ≤ h < 160.

### Situation: a two-way table

**Example.** In a class of 30, 16 are girls. 11 girls and 6 boys walk to school; the rest come by bus.

| | Walk | Bus | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Girls | 11 | 5 | 16 |
| Boys | 6 | 8 | 14 |
| Total | 17 | 13 | 30 |

## Common mistakes

- Overlapping or missing response boxes in a questionnaire.
- Treating a convenience sample of friends as representative.
- Leaving gaps between bars for continuous data, or no gaps for categorical data.
- Forgetting to check that pie-chart angles add up to 360°.
- Putting a boundary value (such as 160) in the wrong class.
