# Chapter 12 — Probability

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 7 foundations: the probability scale, equally likely outcomes, listing outcomes, and experimental probability. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### The probability scale

Probability measures how likely an event is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). 1/2 is an even chance. Probabilities can be written as fractions, decimals or percentages: 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%. No probability is less than 0 or more than 1.

### Equally likely outcomes

When every outcome is equally likely, P(event) = number of outcomes in the event ÷ total number of outcomes. A fair dice has 6 equally likely outcomes, so P(rolling a 5) = 1/6.

### The probability of an event not happening

An event either happens or it does not, so P(not A) = 1 − P(A). If the probability of rain is 0.35, the probability of no rain is 0.65.

### Listing outcomes

For two events together, list every combination in an organised way, or in a table. Two coins give HH, HT, TH and TT: four equally likely outcomes. HT and TH are different outcomes.

### Experimental probability

When the outcomes are not equally likely, or you cannot tell, do an experiment. The experimental probability is the number of times the event happened ÷ the number of trials. More trials give a more reliable estimate.

## Key facts and formulas

| Fact or method | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1 | From impossible to certain | Checking any probability. |
| P(A) = outcomes in A ÷ total outcomes | Equally likely outcomes | Dice, coins, cards and bags. |
| P(not A) = 1 − P(A) | The complement | The chance something does not happen. |
| Experimental P = times it happened ÷ trials | Relative frequency | Results of experiments. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: a bag of counters

**Example.** A bag holds 3 red, 5 blue and 2 green counters, 10 in all. P(blue) = 5/10 = 1/2.

**Example — not red.** P(not red) = 1 − 3/10 = 7/10.

**Example — impossible.** There are no yellow counters, so P(yellow) = 0.

### Situation: rolling a fair dice

**Example.** P(an even number) = 3/6 = 1/2, because 2, 4 and 6 are even.

**Example — more than 4.** P(more than 4) = 2/6 = 1/3, because only 5 and 6 are more than 4.

**Example — a prime number.** 2, 3 and 5 are prime, so P(prime) = 3/6 = 1/2.

### Situation: two events

**Example — two coins.** The outcomes are HH, HT, TH and TT. P(one head and one tail) = 2/4 = 1/2.

**Example — a spinner and a coin.** A spinner shows 1, 2 or 3, and a coin shows H or T. The six outcomes are 1H, 1T, 2H, 2T, 3H and 3T, so P(3 and a head) = 1/6.

### Situation: experiments

**Example — experimental probability.** A drawing pin lands point up 36 times in 60 throws. The experimental probability of point up is 36/60 = 0.6.

**Example — more trials.** After 300 throws it has landed point up 171 times: 171/300 = 0.57. The estimate from 300 throws is more reliable than the one from 60.

**Example — comparing with theory.** A fair coin lands heads 46 times in 100 throws. The experimental probability is 46/100 = 0.46, close to the theoretical 1/2 = 0.5.

### Situation: the complement

**Example.** The probability that a train is late is 0.15. The probability it is not late is 1 − 0.15 = 0.85.

## Common mistakes

- Giving a probability bigger than 1, or negative.
- Missing outcomes when listing: with two coins, HT and TH are both outcomes.
- Thinking a fair dice "remembers": after three sixes, the next roll is still 1/6 to be a six.
- Trusting a few trials: 2 heads in 3 throws does not mean P(head) is 2/3; more trials give a better estimate.
- Writing a probability as a ratio, such as 1 : 5, instead of a fraction, decimal or percentage.
