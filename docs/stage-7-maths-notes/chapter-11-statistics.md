# Chapter 11 — Statistics and data

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 7 foundations: types of data, frequency tables, averages and range, charts, and comparing sets of data. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### Types of data

Categorical data are words or categories, such as favourite colour. Discrete data are counts, such as the number of pets. Continuous data are measurements, such as height or time, which can take any value in a range.

### Frequency tables

A tally chart or frequency table records how often each value occurs. Large sets of numbers are often grouped into class intervals, such as 0–9, 10–19 and 20–29.

### The three averages

- The mode is the most common value.
- The median is the middle value once the data are in order. With an even number of values, it is halfway between the middle two.
- The mean is the total of the values divided by how many values there are.

### The range

The range is the largest value minus the smallest. It measures how spread out the data are: a small range means the data are consistent.

### Charts

Bar charts compare frequencies, with gaps between the bars for categorical data. Pictograms use symbols, with a key. In a pie chart the whole circle, 360°, stands for the total, so each item gets (frequency ÷ total) × 360°. Line graphs show how something changes over time.

### Comparing sets of data

Compare an average (which set is higher?) and the range (which set is more consistent?), and say what each means in context.

## Key facts and formulas

| Fact or method | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Mode = most common value | The value, not how often it occurs | Categorical data and quick summaries. |
| Median = middle value in order | Order the data first | When a few extreme values would distort the mean. |
| Mean = total ÷ number of values | Share the total equally | Using every value. |
| Range = largest − smallest | Spread | Measuring consistency. |
| Pie chart angle = frequency ÷ total × 360° | Each item's share of the circle | Drawing pie charts. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: averages and range

**Example.** For 4, 7, 2, 9, 7, 5: the mode is 7. In order the data are 2, 4, 5, 7, 7, 9, so the median is halfway between 5 and 7, which is 6. The total is 34, so the mean is 34 ÷ 6 = 5.666..., about 5.7. The range is 9 − 2 = 7.

**Example — a missing value.** Five numbers have a mean of 8, so their total is 5 × 8 = 40. Four of them are 6, 9, 7 and 11, which add to 33. The fifth number is 40 − 33 = 7.

### Situation: frequency tables

**Example.** A team scored 0 goals in 3 matches, 1 goal in 5 matches, 2 goals in 4 matches and 3 goals in 2 matches: 14 matches.

- Total goals = 0 × 3 + 1 × 5 + 2 × 4 + 3 × 2 = 0 + 5 + 8 + 6 = 19, so the mean is 19 ÷ 14 = 1.357..., about 1.4 goals.
- The mode is 1 goal (5 matches).
- The median is halfway between the 7th and 8th values. The first 3 values are 0 and the next 5 are 1, so the 7th and 8th values are both 1, and the median is 1.

### Situation: comparing two classes

**Example.** Class A has a mean test score of 62 with a range of 30. Class B has a mean of 58 with a range of 12. Class A did better on average, but Class B's scores were more consistent.

### Situation: charts

**Example — a pie chart.** 12 of 30 students chose football. The football sector is 12 ÷ 30 × 360° = 144°.

**Example — checking a pie chart.** Sectors of 144°, 96°, 72° and 48° add up to 360°, so none is missing.

## Common mistakes

- Finding the median without putting the data in order first.
- Giving the frequency as the mode: the mode is the value that occurs most, not how many times it occurs.
- Dividing by the number of rows in a frequency table instead of the total frequency when finding the mean.
- Giving the largest value as the range instead of largest minus smallest.
- Pie chart angles that do not add up to 360°.
