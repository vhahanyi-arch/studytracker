# Chapter 4 — Powers and roots

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 7 foundations: square and cube numbers, their roots, and index notation. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### Square numbers

A square number is a number multiplied by itself: 7² = 7 × 7 = 49. The small 2 is the index; read 7² as "7 squared". The first square numbers are 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144.

### Cube numbers

A cube number is a number used three times in a multiplication: 4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. Read it as "4 cubed". The first cube numbers are 1, 8, 27, 64, 125.

### Square roots and cube roots

A square root undoes squaring: √49 = 7 because 7² = 49. A cube root undoes cubing: ∛64 = 4 because 4³ = 64. (−7)² is also 49, but the √ sign means the positive root.

### Index notation

An index tells you how many times the number is used in the multiplication: 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32. Powers of 10 count the zeros: 10³ = 1000 and 10⁴ = 10000.

### Estimating roots

A number that is not a square has a square root between two whole numbers. 50 lies between the squares 49 and 64, so √50 is between 7 and 8, and closer to 7.

### Powers in the order of operations

Powers and roots come after brackets but before multiplication and division: 2 × 3² = 2 × 9 = 18, whereas (2 × 3)² = 6² = 36.

## Key facts and formulas

| Fact or method | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| n² = n × n | n squared | Squares and areas of squares. |
| n³ = n × n × n | n cubed | Cubes and volumes of cubes. |
| √(n²) = n, ∛(n³) = n | Roots undo powers | Finding a side from an area or volume. |
| 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144, 169, 196, 225 | The squares of 1 to 15 | Recognising squares and roots. |
| 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 1000 | The cubes of 1 to 5, and 10 | Recognising cubes and cube roots. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: working out powers

**Example.** 2⁴ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16.

**Example — a sum of squares.** 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25.

**Example — a power of 10.** 10⁴ = 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10000.

### Situation: finding roots

**Example.** √81 = 9 and ∛125 = 5.

**Example — work inside the root first.** √(16 + 9) = √25 = 5.

**Example — estimating.** √30 is between 5 and 6, because 25 < 30 < 36. It is closer to 5.

### Situation: powers in a calculation

**Example.** 5 + 2³ = 5 + 8 = 13.

**Example.** 20 − √36 = 20 − 6 = 14.

**Example — brackets change the order.** 2 × 3² = 18, but (2 × 3)² = 36.

### Situation: area and volume

**Example — side from an area.** A square garden has an area of 64 m². Its side is √64 = 8 m.

**Example — edge from a volume.** A cube has a volume of 27 cm³. Its edge is ∛27 = 3 cm.

**Example — area from a side.** A square tile has sides of 30 cm. Its area is 30² = 900 cm².

## Common mistakes

- Doubling instead of squaring: 4² is 16, not 8.
- Multiplying by 3 instead of cubing: 2³ is 8, not 6.
- Splitting a root over a sum: √(9 + 16) is √25 = 5, not 3 + 4 = 7.
- Squaring before multiplying when brackets say otherwise, or the other way round: 2 × 3² is 18, not 36.
- Forgetting that a negative number squared is positive: (−3)² = 9.
