# Chapter 10 — Position and transformations

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 7 foundations: coordinates in four quadrants, symmetry, reflections, translations and rotations. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### Coordinates in four quadrants

A point is written (x, y): x is how far across, y is how far up. Negative x is to the left of the y-axis and negative y is below the x-axis. A(3, −2) is 3 to the right and 2 down. Any point on the y-axis has x = 0, and any point on the x-axis has y = 0.

### Line symmetry and rotational symmetry

A shape has a line of symmetry if folding along the line makes the two halves match exactly. Its order of rotational symmetry is the number of times it looks the same during one full turn. A shape that only matches itself after a full turn has rotational symmetry of order 1.

### Reflection

A reflection flips a shape over a mirror line. Each point of the image is the same distance from the mirror line as the original point, on the opposite side. Reflecting in the x-axis changes the sign of y; reflecting in the y-axis changes the sign of x.

### Translation

A translation slides a shape without turning or flipping it. Describe it by how far it moves right or left and up or down, such as "5 right and 3 down".

### Rotation

A rotation turns a shape about a fixed point, the centre of rotation. Describe it by the angle (such as 90° or 180°), the direction (clockwise or anticlockwise) and the centre. Rotating 180° about the origin changes the sign of both coordinates.

### Congruent images

Reflections, translations and rotations change position but not shape or size: the image is congruent to the original.

## Key facts and formulas

| Fact or method | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| (x, y): across, then up | The order of coordinates | Plotting and reading points. |
| Reflect in x-axis: (x, y) → (x, −y) | y changes sign | Reflections in the x-axis. |
| Reflect in y-axis: (x, y) → (−x, y) | x changes sign | Reflections in the y-axis. |
| Rotate 180° about the origin: (x, y) → (−x, −y) | Both signs change | Half-turns about (0, 0). |
| Rotate 90° clockwise about the origin: (x, y) → (y, −x) | A quarter turn | Quarter turns about (0, 0). |
| Angle, direction and centre | Everything a rotation needs | Describing a rotation fully. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: coordinates

**Example — a missing corner.** A square has corners at (1, 1), (4, 1) and (4, 4). The fourth corner is (1, 4).

**Example — quadrants.** (−3, 2) is in the top-left quadrant, and (−3, −2) is in the bottom-left quadrant.

### Situation: reflections

**Example — in the axes.** Reflecting (2, 5) in the x-axis gives (2, −5); reflecting it in the y-axis gives (−2, 5).

**Example — in a vertical line.** Reflect (1, 2) in the line x = 3. The point is 2 to the left of the line, so its image is 2 to the right: (5, 2).

### Situation: translations

**Example.** Translating (−1, 4) 5 right and 3 down gives (4, 1).

**Example — describing one.** (2, 3) moves to (−1, 7). x goes down by 3 and y goes up by 4, so the translation is 3 left and 4 up.

### Situation: rotations

**Example — a half turn.** Rotating (3, 1) through 180° about the origin gives (−3, −1).

**Example — a quarter turn.** Rotating (3, 1) through 90° clockwise about the origin gives (1, −3).

### Situation: symmetry

**Example — shapes.** A rectangle has 2 lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order 2. A square has 4 lines and order 4. An equilateral triangle has 3 lines and order 3.

**Example — no lines, but turns.** A parallelogram (not a rectangle) has no lines of symmetry but rotational symmetry of order 2.

**Example — a letter.** The capital letter H has 2 lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order 2.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the coordinates the wrong way round: x comes first.
- Reflecting in the wrong axis: reflecting in the x-axis changes y, not x.
- Rotating the wrong way, or about the wrong centre.
- Describing a rotation without its angle, direction and centre.
- Turning a shape when translating it: a translation only slides.
