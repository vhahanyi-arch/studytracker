# Chapter 3 — Ratio and proportion

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 7 foundations: writing and simplifying ratios, sharing in a ratio, and direct proportion. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### What a ratio says

A ratio compares the sizes of parts. If a class has 3 boys for every 2 girls, the ratio of boys to girls is 3 : 2. The order matters: girls to boys is 2 : 3.

### Simplifying ratios

Divide every part by the same number, ideally the highest common factor: 12 : 18 = 2 : 3 (divide by 6). The parts must be in the same units first: 50 cm : 2 m = 50 cm : 200 cm = 1 : 4.

### Ratios and fractions

A ratio of 3 : 2 has 3 + 2 = 5 parts in total, so 3/5 of the class are boys and 2/5 are girls. A ratio compares part with part; a fraction compares part with whole.

### Sharing in a ratio

To share an amount in a ratio, add the parts, find the value of one part, then multiply. To share 35 dollars in the ratio 2 : 5: there are 7 parts, one part is 35 ÷ 7 = 5 dollars, so the shares are 10 and 25 dollars.

### Direct proportion

Two quantities are in direct proportion when doubling one doubles the other: twice as many pens cost twice as much. The unitary method finds the value of one first, then multiplies.

### Rates and best buys

A rate compares two different quantities, such as cost per item or kilometres per hour. To find the better buy, work out the price of one item (or one gram) for each option.

## Key facts and formulas

| Fact or method | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| a : b = (a × k) : (b × k) | Multiplying or dividing every part gives an equivalent ratio | Simplifying and scaling. |
| One part = total ÷ number of parts | The first step in sharing | Sharing an amount in a ratio. |
| In a : b, the first part is a/(a + b) of the whole | Ratio to fraction | Finding what fraction one part is. |
| Value of one, then multiply | The unitary method | Recipes, prices and rates. |
| Price ÷ quantity | Cost of one unit | Comparing best buys. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: simplifying ratios

**Example.** 45 : 60 = 3 : 4 (divide both by 15).

**Example — different units.** 2 kg : 500 g = 2000 g : 500 g = 4 : 1.

**Example — three parts.** 6 : 9 : 15 = 2 : 3 : 5 (divide each by 3).

### Situation: sharing in a ratio

**Example.** Share 48 sweets in the ratio 1 : 3. There are 4 parts; one part is 48 ÷ 4 = 12. The shares are 12 and 36 sweets.

**Example — knowing one share.** Boys and girls are in the ratio 3 : 4 and there are 12 boys. One part is 12 ÷ 3 = 4, so there are 4 × 4 = 16 girls.

**Example — checking.** Sharing 35 dollars in the ratio 2 : 5 gives 10 and 25 dollars; 10 + 25 = 35, so the shares are right.

### Situation: direct proportion

**Example — prices.** 5 pens cost 3.50 dollars. One pen costs 3.50 ÷ 5 = 0.70 dollars, so 8 pens cost 8 × 0.70 = 5.60 dollars.

**Example — a recipe.** A recipe for 4 people uses 300 g of flour. For 6 people: 300 ÷ 4 = 75 g per person, and 75 × 6 = 450 g.

**Example — a rate.** A car travels 150 km in 3 hours at a steady speed. In one hour it travels 50 km, so in 5 hours it travels 250 km.

### Situation: best buys

**Example.** A box of 6 eggs costs 1.80 dollars and a box of 10 costs 2.70 dollars. Per egg: 1.80 ÷ 6 = 0.30 and 2.70 ÷ 10 = 0.27 dollars. The box of 10 is the better buy.

**Example — by weight.** 500 g of rice costs 2 dollars and 2 kg costs 7 dollars. Per kilogram: 4 dollars and 3.50 dollars, so the 2 kg bag is better value.

## Common mistakes

- Writing the parts in the wrong order: "boys to girls" puts boys first.
- Simplifying without changing to the same units first.
- Dividing the amount by one part of the ratio instead of the total number of parts.
- Adding on instead of scaling: for 6 people instead of 4, the flour is not "a bit more than 300 g"; find one person's share and multiply: 300 ÷ 4 × 6 = 450 g.
- Mixing up a ratio and a fraction: in 3 : 2 the boys are 3/5 of the class, not 3/2.
