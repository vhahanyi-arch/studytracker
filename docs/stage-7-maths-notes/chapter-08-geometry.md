# Chapter 8 — Angles and geometrical reasoning

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 7 foundations: types of angle, angle facts, properties of triangles and quadrilaterals, and constructing triangles. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### Types of angle

An acute angle is less than 90°, a right angle is exactly 90°, an obtuse angle is between 90° and 180°, and a reflex angle is more than 180°. Measure with a protractor: put its centre on the vertex, line up 0° with one arm, and read from the scale that starts at 0 on that arm.

### Angles on a line and around a point

Angles on a straight line add up to 180°. Angles around a point add up to 360°. When two straight lines cross, the vertically opposite angles are equal.

### Angles in triangles and quadrilaterals

The angles in a triangle add up to 180°. The angles in a quadrilateral add up to 360°, because a diagonal splits it into two triangles.

### Types of triangle

- Equilateral: three equal sides and three 60° angles.
- Isosceles: two equal sides, and the two angles opposite them (the base angles) are equal.
- Scalene: no equal sides or angles.
- Right-angled: one angle of 90°.

### Quadrilaterals

A square has four equal sides and four right angles. A rectangle has four right angles and opposite sides equal. A parallelogram has opposite sides parallel and equal, and opposite angles equal. A rhombus is a parallelogram with four equal sides. A trapezium has one pair of parallel sides. A kite has two pairs of equal adjacent sides.

### Constructing triangles

To draw a triangle from two sides and the angle between them, draw one side, measure the angle at one end with a protractor, mark the second side along it, and join the ends. To draw a triangle from three sides, draw one side, then use compasses to draw arcs of the other two lengths from its ends; the third corner is where the arcs cross.

## Key facts and formulas

| Fact or method | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Angles on a straight line = 180° | They make a half turn | Finding a missing angle on a line. |
| Angles around a point = 360° | They make a full turn | Missing angles at a point. |
| Vertically opposite angles are equal | Crossing lines | Two straight lines crossing. |
| Angles in a triangle = 180° | Always true | Any triangle. |
| Angles in a quadrilateral = 360° | Two triangles | Any four-sided shape. |
| Isosceles: base angles are equal | Two equal sides, two equal angles | Isosceles triangles. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: angles on a line and at a point

**Example — on a line.** One angle on a straight line is 125°. The other is 180° − 125° = 55°.

**Example — around a point.** Three angles around a point are 90°, 130° and x. x = 360° − 90° − 130° = 140°.

**Example — crossing lines.** Two lines cross and one angle is 70°. The angle opposite it is also 70°, and each of the other two is 180° − 70° = 110°.

### Situation: triangles

**Example.** A triangle has angles of 48° and 75°. The third angle is 180° − 48° − 75° = 57°.

**Example — isosceles, top angle given.** The angle between the equal sides is 40°. The base angles share 180° − 40° = 140°, so each is 70°.

**Example — isosceles, base angle given.** Each base angle is 65°. The third angle is 180° − 65° − 65° = 50°.

**Example — right-angled.** One angle is 34°. The other non-right angle is 90° − 34° = 56°.

### Situation: quadrilaterals

**Example.** Three angles of a quadrilateral are 80°, 95° and 110°. The fourth is 360° − 80° − 95° − 110° = 75°.

**Example — a parallelogram.** One angle is 65°. The angle next to it is 180° − 65° = 115°, and opposite angles are equal, so the angles are 65°, 115°, 65° and 115°.

### Situation: reasoning with algebra

**Example.** Two angles on a straight line are 2x and 3x. 2x + 3x = 180°, so 5x = 180° and x = 36°. The angles are 72° and 108°.

### Situation: constructing triangles

**Example — two sides and the angle between.** Draw AB = 7 cm. At A, measure 40° and draw a line. Mark AC = 5 cm on it, then join C to B.

**Example — three sides.** Draw a 6 cm line PQ. Open the compasses to 5 cm and draw an arc from P; open them to 4 cm and draw an arc from Q. Join P and Q to the point R where the arcs cross.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the wrong scale on the protractor, so 50° is read as 130°.
- Assuming angles are equal because they look equal: use the facts, not the drawing.
- Using 180° for a quadrilateral: its angles add up to 360°.
- Making the wrong angles equal in an isosceles triangle: the base angles are the ones opposite the equal sides.
- Forgetting to show the arcs when constructing with compasses.
