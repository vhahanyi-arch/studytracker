# Topic 11 — Particle physics

Cambridge International AS Level Physics 9702, 2025–2027 syllabus, version 1, printed page 25. This reference covers the 18 objectives in 11.1–11.2. The question bank uses text notation `^A_Z X` for a nuclide: A is the upper number and Z the lower number.

## 11.1 Atoms, nuclei and radiation

### Alpha scattering and the nuclear atom — 11.1.1–11.1.2

In the alpha-particle scattering experiment, a beam of alpha particles is directed at thin metal foil. Most pass through with little or no deflection. A small fraction are deflected through large angles, and a very small number return backwards.

Most passing through supports the conclusion that atoms are mostly empty space. Large deflections of massive positive alpha particles require a concentrated, positively charged centre containing most of the atom's mass. The rarity of close encounters shows that this nucleus is very small compared with the atom. A model with positive charge spread throughout the atom cannot account for the observed large deflections.

The simple nuclear model contains positively charged protons and neutral neutrons in a small nucleus, with negatively charged orbital electrons outside it. Do not describe the nucleus as containing the atom's ordinary orbital electrons. In a neutral atom, electron number equals proton number; forming an ion changes electron number without changing the element.

| Particle | Charge | Approximate mass | Location in the simple atom |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | +e | 1 u | Nucleus |
| Neutron | 0 | 1 u | Nucleus |
| Electron | −e | 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, about 1/1836 of a proton mass | Outside nucleus |

Here e is the positive elementary charge, approximately 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. These approximate masses do not imply that proton and neutron masses are exactly equal.

### Numbers, isotopes and nuclide notation — 11.1.3–11.1.5

The proton number Z counts protons and identifies the element. The nucleon number A counts protons plus neutrons. Neutron number N = A − Z. These are dimensionless integer counts, not masses in kilograms.

Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers, and hence different nucleon numbers. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have Z = 6, but have six and eight neutrons respectively. Nuclei with equal A but different Z are different elements, not isotopes of one element.

Write a nuclide as `^A_Z X`, where X is the chemical symbol. For example, `^14_6 C` has six protons and eight neutrons. Its neutral atom has six electrons. The upper number is not the proton number, and the lower number does not count neutrons.

### Conservation and radiation — 11.1.6–11.1.8

In a nuclear equation, total nucleon number and total electric charge are conserved. Balance the upper numbers and the signed lower numbers separately. For nuclear species the lower number is proton number; for an emitted beta particle it represents charge in units of e. Thus `^0_-1 e` has charge −e and nucleon number zero. Its zero upper number does not mean zero mass.

| Radiation | Composition | Rest mass | Charge | Equation notation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha (α) | Helium-4 nucleus: two protons and two neutrons | Approximately 4 u | +2e | `^4_2 α` |
| Beta-minus (β⁻) | Electron | m_e | −e | `^0_-1 e` |
| Beta-plus (β⁺) | Positron | m_e | +e | `^0_+1 e` |
| Gamma (γ) | Photon of electromagnetic radiation | Zero | Zero | `^0_0 γ` |

Gamma photons carry energy despite having zero rest mass. An antiparticle has the same mass and opposite electric charge to its corresponding particle. The positron is the electron's antiparticle: it has mass m_e and charge +e. Antimatter does not have negative mass. Charge reversal alone cannot distinguish neutral particles from their antiparticles.

### Beta partners and energy spectra — 11.1.9–11.1.10

Beta-minus decay emits an electron and an electron antineutrino. Beta-plus decay emits a positron and an electron neutrino. Both neutral partners have zero charge and zero nucleon number; these statements do not assign a zero rest mass to a neutrino.

For a specified alpha transition between parent and daughter states, the energy release is fixed and is shared in a fixed way between the alpha particle and the recoiling daughter. Alpha energies are therefore discrete. More than one transition can produce more than one discrete energy; it is incorrect to claim that all alpha particles from all decays have one universal energy.

Beta particles have a continuous range of kinetic energies because the available energy is shared in varying proportions with the emitted neutrino or antineutrino and the recoiling daughter. Total energy remains conserved. The continuous beta spectrum does not mean that energy conservation fails.

### Decay equations — 11.1.11

Use the same notation on both sides of each equation. In the expressions below, ν_e denotes an electron neutrino and anti-ν_e an electron antineutrino; each has A = 0 and charge number 0.

| Mode | General equation | Change in daughter nucleus |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha | `^A_Z X → ^(A−4)_(Z−2) Y + ^4_2 α` | A decreases by 4; Z decreases by 2 |
| Beta-minus | `^A_Z X → ^A_(Z+1) Y + ^0_-1 e + ^0_0 anti-ν_e` | A unchanged; Z increases by 1 |
| Beta-plus | `^A_Z X → ^A_(Z−1) Y + ^0_+1 e + ^0_0 ν_e` | A unchanged; Z decreases by 1 |

Examples:

- `^238_92 U → ^234_90 Th + ^4_2 α`: 238 = 234 + 4 and 92 = 90 + 2.
- `^14_6 C → ^14_7 N + ^0_-1 e + ^0_0 anti-ν_e`: 14 = 14 + 0 + 0 and 6 = 7 − 1 + 0.
- `^11_6 C → ^11_5 B + ^0_+1 e + ^0_0 ν_e`: 11 = 11 + 0 + 0 and 6 = 5 + 1 + 0.

The daughter symbol must match its new Z. In beta decay, the electron or positron is produced during the transformation; it is not an orbital electron escaping the atom. Conservation balances are necessary checks, but do not by themselves establish whether a proposed decay is energetically possible. The question bank uses specified, known decay examples.

### Unified atomic mass unit — 11.1.12

The unified atomic mass unit u is one twelfth of the mass of a free, neutral carbon-12 atom at rest in its ground state. Use the supplied conversion, approximately 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Here u denotes a unit; in a quark composition such as uud, the letter u instead denotes an up quark.

Example: a mass reported as 12 u is 12 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 19.92 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 1.992 × 10⁻²⁶ kg using that conversion. If a question asks for the coefficient multiplying 10⁻²⁷ kg, enter 19.92. A mass of approximately A u is an estimate, not an exact identity between nucleon count and measured nuclear mass.

## 11.2 Fundamental particles

### Quark flavours and charges — 11.2.1–11.2.2

A quark is a fundamental particle: it is not described here as a composite of smaller constituents. There are six flavours. Each antiquark has the opposite charge to its corresponding quark.

| Flavour | Symbol | Quark charge | Antiquark charge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Up | u | +2e/3 | −2e/3 |
| Down | d | −e/3 | +e/3 |
| Strange | s | −e/3 | +e/3 |
| Charm | c | +2e/3 | −2e/3 |
| Top | t | +2e/3 | −2e/3 |
| Bottom | b | −e/3 | +e/3 |

An overbar denotes an antiquark, for example anti-d (d with a bar). In units of e/3, an up quark has charge +2 and an anti-down quark has charge +1. These integer coefficients do not mean the charges are whole multiples of e. No other properties of the six flavours are required by these objectives.

### Nucleons and hadrons — 11.2.3–11.2.4

A proton has quark composition uud, giving charge +2e/3 + 2e/3 − e/3 = +e. A neutron is udd, giving +2e/3 − e/3 − e/3 = 0. Both are composite, even though they are constituents of a nucleus.

Hadrons contain quarks. In the classification required here, a baryon consists of three quarks and a meson consists of one quark and one antiquark. Protons and neutrons are baryons. An up quark paired with a down antiquark is a meson with total charge +e. A particle's charge alone does not determine whether it is a baryon, meson or lepton; examine its constituents.

### Quark changes in beta decay — 11.2.5

In beta-minus decay, a neutron becomes a proton: udd → uud. One down quark becomes an up quark, with an electron and electron antineutrino emitted. Charge is conserved: −e/3 = +2e/3 − e + 0.

In nuclear beta-plus decay, a proton becomes a neutron: uud → udd. One up quark becomes a down quark, with a positron and electron neutrino emitted. Charge is conserved: +2e/3 = −e/3 + e + 0. This statement concerns an energetically allowed nuclear decay; it does not claim that an isolated proton spontaneously undergoes this decay.

The other two constituent quarks need not change flavour. A quark does not become its own antiquark in either of these beta transformations.

### Leptons — 11.2.6

Electrons and neutrinos are fundamental particles called leptons. They are not made of quarks and are not hadrons. An electron or neutrino emitted in a nuclear process remains a lepton: classification is not determined by its place of production. Positrons and antineutrinos are the corresponding antiparticles.

## Formula and conservation sheet

This topic uses particle relationships and conservation equations more than numerical formulas. The symbolic decay equations above are part of this sheet.

| Relationship | Symbol meanings, units and conditions |
|---|---|
| A = Z + N; N = A − Z | A: nucleon number; Z: proton number; N: neutron number. Dimensionless non-negative integer counts, with A ≥ Z. |
| Nuclear charge q = Ze | q: nuclear charge in coulombs (C); e: positive elementary charge, approximately 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Applies to a nucleus, not a neutral atom. |
| Neutral-atom electron count = Z | Count is dimensionless. Applies only to a neutral atom. |
| ΣA_before = ΣA_after | Sum of nucleon numbers in the nuclear processes studied here. Beta particles, photons and (anti)neutrinos contribute zero. |
| Σq_before = Σq_after | Charge q in C, or consistently in units of e. Keep signs for beta particles and antiquarks. |
| m = μ m_u; μ = m/m_u | m: mass in kg; μ: dimensionless numerical mass value in u; m_u: mass represented by one u, approximately 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Use the given precision. |
| q_total = Σq_i | Total charge and each constituent charge q_i in C, or matching multiples of e; used for quark composites. |
| q_antiparticle = −q_particle; m_antiparticle = m_particle | Electric charges q in C; rest masses m in kg or matching mass units. Opposite charge does not imply negative mass. |
| n → p + e⁻ + anti-ν_e | Beta-minus particle transformation; n: neutron, p: proton, e⁻: electron. Charge 0 = +e − e + 0; nucleon count 1 = 1 + 0 + 0. |
| p → n + e⁺ + ν_e | Transformation within an allowed beta-plus nuclear decay; e⁺: positron. Charge +e = 0 + e + 0; nucleon count 1 = 1 + 0 + 0. |
| d → u + e⁻ + anti-ν_e; u → d + e⁺ + ν_e | Corresponding single-quark changes in nuclear beta decay; u and d mean up and down quarks, not units. |

For exam practice, explain the scattering evidence, write and balance decay equations, identify a neutrino versus antineutrino, and use constituent charges to justify a particle classification. Avoid treating zero nucleon number as zero mass or assuming that neutral means fundamental.
