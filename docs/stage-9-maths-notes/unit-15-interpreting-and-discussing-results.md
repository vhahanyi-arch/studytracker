# Unit 15 — Interpreting and discussing results

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 9, objectives 9Ss.04 and 9Ss.05. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### Comparing distributions, including grouped data — 9Ss.04

Compare two sets of data with an average (which is larger on the whole) and the range (which is more consistent), and write each comparison in context.

With grouped data you do not know the exact values, so:

- the **modal class** is the class with the highest frequency;
- the **class containing the median** is found from the running total;
- the **mean is estimated** using the midpoint of each class: estimated mean = Σ(midpoint × frequency) ÷ Σfrequency;
- the **range is estimated** as the highest possible value minus the lowest possible value.

These are estimates because the midpoints stand in for the real values.

### Interpreting data and misleading information — 9Ss.05

- Make inferences about the population only as far as the sample allows: consider its size and how it was chosen.
- Correlation does not prove cause: ice-cream sales and sunburn rise together because both depend on sunny weather.
- Watch for misleading presentation: a vertical axis not starting at zero, uneven scales, pictures that grow in two directions, 3D charts that distort sizes, missing labels or units, and percentages without the totals behind them.
- Give the evidence for every conclusion, and say what further data would help.

## Key facts and formulas

| Formula | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Estimated mean = Σ(midpoint × f) ÷ Σf | Mean from grouped data | Grouped frequency tables. |
| Midpoint = (lower + upper class bound) ÷ 2 | Stand-in for every value in a class | Estimating the mean. |
| Median position = (n + 1) ÷ 2 | Which value is the median | Finding the median or its class. |
| Modal class = class with the highest frequency | Most common group | Grouped data. |
| Compare an average and the range, in context | Two-part comparison | Comparing distributions. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: estimating from grouped data

**Example.** Times (minutes) for 20 students to solve a puzzle:

| Time t | 0 ≤ t < 10 | 10 ≤ t < 20 | 20 ≤ t < 30 | 30 ≤ t < 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 4 | 9 | 5 | 2 |

- Midpoints: 5, 15, 25, 35. Σ(midpoint × f) = 20 + 135 + 125 + 70 = 350.
- Estimated mean = 350 ÷ 20 = 17.5 minutes.
- Modal class: 10 ≤ t < 20.
- Median: the 10th and 11th values. Running totals 4, 13: both are in 10 ≤ t < 20.
- Estimated range: at most 40 − 0 = 40 minutes.

### Situation: comparing two groups

**Example.** Group A's estimated mean time is 17.5 minutes with range 40; group B's is 21 minutes with range 25. On average group A was faster (lower mean); group B's times were more consistent (smaller range).

### Situation: choosing an average

**Example.** House prices in a street: $150 000, $160 000, $155 000, $165 000 and $900 000. The median ($160 000) represents a typical house; the mean ($306 000) is pulled up by one very expensive house.

### Situation: spotting misleading information

**Example — axis.** A graph of a school's pass rate goes from 81% to 84%, but the vertical axis starts at 80%, so the bar for 84% looks four times as tall as 81%. The real change is 3 percentage points.

**Example — percentages without totals.** "Sales up 50%!" may mean an increase from 2 to 3 items.

### Situation: correlation and cause

**Example.** Towns with more fire stations have more fires. This does not mean fire stations cause fires: larger towns have both more fire stations and more fires.

### Situation: inference from a sample

**Example.** 30 students from one class are asked how long they spend on homework. Their mean is 1.5 hours a night. It is not safe to say every student in the country does about 1.5 hours: the sample is small and from one class. It does give evidence about that class.

## Common mistakes

- Using class boundaries or frequencies instead of midpoints for the estimated mean.
- Dividing by the number of classes instead of the total frequency.
- Saying the estimated mean is exact.
- Treating correlation as proof of cause.
- Comparing two groups without referring to the context (minutes, marks, prices).
