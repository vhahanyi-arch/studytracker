# Unit 6 — Statistical investigations

Cambridge Lower Secondary Mathematics, Stage 9, objectives 9Ss.01, 9Ss.02 and 9Ss.03. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Key ideas

### Planning: what data to collect — 9Ss.01

A statistical investigation starts with a question or hypothesis ("Students who sleep more score higher in tests"). Then decide what data to collect, from whom, how much, and how.

Types of data:

- **qualitative:** described in words (colour, opinion, type of transport);
- **quantitative:** numbers, either **discrete** (counted, separate values: number of pets) or **continuous** (measured, any value in a range: time, height, mass);
- **categorical:** sorted into groups or categories; qualitative data is usually categorical.

### Bias in collecting data — 9Ss.02

Bias means the results lean one way and do not represent the population. Common causes:

- a sample that is not representative (asking only people at a gym how often they exercise);
- a sample that is too small;
- leading or unclear questions ("Do you agree that the new park is a waste of money?");
- non-response: the people who choose to reply may differ from those who do not;
- the time or place of collecting (a traffic survey only at 3 a.m.).

Reduce bias with random sampling, a larger sample, neutral wording, and collecting at different times and places.

### Recording and representing data — 9Ss.03

Choose a table and a diagram that suit the data and the question:

- frequency tables, grouped with inequalities for continuous data (20 ≤ t < 30);
- two-way tables for two categories at once;
- bar charts and pie charts for categorical data;
- frequency diagrams (bars touching) and frequency polygons for grouped continuous data; a frequency polygon joins the points plotted at the midpoints of the classes;
- stem-and-leaf diagrams (including back-to-back) to keep the raw values while showing the shape;
- scatter graphs for two numerical variables, to look for correlation;
- line graphs for change over time.

## Key facts and formulas

| Fact or formula | What it means | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Class midpoint = (lower bound + upper bound) ÷ 2 | Middle of a class | Plotting frequency polygons; estimating a mean. |
| Pie angle = frequency ÷ total × 360° | Share of the circle | Pie charts. |
| Positive, negative or no correlation | Direction of the trend on a scatter graph | Two numerical variables. |
| Random sample, larger sample, neutral questions | Ways to reduce bias | Planning data collection. |

## Using the methods in different situations

### Situation: classifying data

**Example.** Favourite music genre: qualitative, categorical. Number of goals scored: quantitative, discrete. Time to finish a puzzle: quantitative, continuous.

### Situation: spotting and fixing bias

**Example.** To find out how many hours students in a school spend on homework, Leila hands a questionnaire to the 15 students in the homework club. This is biased: the homework club is not representative, and 15 is small. Better: a random sample of about 50 students from the whole school register.

**Example — a leading question.** "Don't you think school should start later?" pushes people to agree. Better: "What time would you prefer school to start?" with time options that cover all choices.

### Situation: a grouped frequency table and polygon

**Example.** Times (minutes) to complete a task: 0 ≤ t < 10: 4; 10 ≤ t < 20: 9; 20 ≤ t < 30: 5; 30 ≤ t < 40: 2. For a frequency polygon, plot (5, 4), (15, 9), (25, 5) and (35, 2) and join them with straight lines.

### Situation: a back-to-back stem-and-leaf diagram

**Example.** Test marks of two classes can be shown on one stem (tens) with leaves (units) for one class to the left and the other to the right. It keeps every value, so medians and ranges can be read off, and the two shapes can be compared at a glance.

### Situation: choosing a diagram

**Example.** To show how 120 students travel to school, a pie chart or bar chart suits (categorical). To compare students' heights with their arm spans, a scatter graph suits (two numerical variables). To show a town's temperature each month, a line graph suits (change over time).

### Situation: describing correlation

**Example.** A scatter graph of hours of revision against test score shows points rising from left to right: positive correlation. Students who revised more tended to score higher. (This does not prove that revising caused the higher scores.)

## Common mistakes

- Calling continuous data discrete because it was rounded (a time of 12 s is still continuous).
- Using overlapping classes such as 10–20 and 20–30 without inequalities.
- Plotting a frequency polygon at the class boundaries instead of the midpoints.
- Assuming a larger sample removes bias even when it is chosen in a biased way.
- Saying correlation proves cause.
