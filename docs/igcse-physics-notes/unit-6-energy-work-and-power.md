# Unit 6 — Energy, work and power

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 1.7.1, 1.7.2, 1.7.3 and 1.7.4, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Energy stores and transfers — 1.7.1

Energy can be stored as kinetic, gravitational potential, chemical, elastic (strain), nuclear, electrostatic and internal (thermal) energy.

Energy is transferred between stores in four ways:

- by forces doing mechanical work (a crane lifting a load);
- by electrical currents doing electrical work (a battery driving a motor);
- by heating (a hot plate warming a pan);
- by waves: electromagnetic, sound and others (sunlight warming the ground, a loudspeaker).

**Examples of transfers.** A falling ball: gravitational potential → kinetic. A car braking: kinetic → internal (in the hot brakes). A battery-powered torch: chemical → (electrical work) → light and internal. A bow and arrow: elastic → kinetic.

### Conservation of energy — 1.7.1

Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one store to another. The total energy after any event equals the total before. Some energy always ends up as "wasted" internal energy of the surroundings, often through friction or electrical resistance; it is not lost, just spread out and no longer useful.

A flow (Sankey) diagram shows this: the width of each arrow is proportional to the energy. The input arrow is as wide as all the output arrows together. **Supplement:** apply this to processes with several stages, such as a power station (chemical → internal → kinetic of turbine → electrical), and read values off a Sankey diagram drawn to scale.

### Kinetic and gravitational potential energy — 1.7.1 (Supplement)

Kinetic energy depends on mass and the square of speed: double the speed and the kinetic energy becomes four times as large. The change in gravitational potential energy depends on mass, g and the change in height.

When an object falls with negligible air resistance, the loss in gravitational potential energy equals the gain in kinetic energy. When there is resistance, part of the potential energy becomes internal energy instead.

### Work — 1.7.2

Work done is the energy transferred. Mechanical work is done when a force moves an object in the direction of the force. Electrical work is done when a current transfers energy. 1 joule is the work done when a 1 N force moves an object 1 m in the direction of the force.

No work is done if there is no movement in the direction of the force: holding a heavy bag still does no work on the bag.

### Energy resources — 1.7.3

| Resource | How the energy is obtained | Renewable? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) | Burned to boil water in a boiler; steam turns a turbine, which turns a generator | No | Reliable and available on demand; release carbon dioxide (climate change) and other pollutants. |
| Biofuels (wood, ethanol, biogas) | Burned like fossil fuels | Yes | Plants absorb carbon dioxide as they grow; need large areas of land. |
| Hydroelectric dams | Water stored behind a dam flows down through turbines | Yes | Reliable, quick to start; floods valleys and damages habitats; needs suitable sites. |
| Tides | Tidal water flows through turbines in a barrage | Yes | Predictable, twice a day; few suitable estuaries, affects wildlife. |
| Waves | Up-and-down motion of waves drives a generator | Yes | Small scale, unreliable (depends on weather), can be a hazard to boats. |
| Geothermal | Hot rocks underground heat water to steam, which turns a turbine | Yes | Reliable; only in volcanic areas; drilling is costly. |
| Nuclear fuel | Fission of uranium heats water to steam for a turbine | No | Large scale and reliable, no carbon dioxide; radioactive waste, risk of accidents, costly to build and decommission. |
| Solar cells | Light is converted directly to electrical energy | Yes | No moving parts, no pollution in use; nothing at night, less on cloudy days, small output per area. |
| Solar panels | Infrared from the Sun heats water flowing through them | Yes | Good for hot water; depends on sunshine. |
| Wind | Wind turns turbine blades connected to a generator | Yes | No fuel or pollution in use; unreliable, noisy, visual impact, many needed. |

Efficiency is how much of the input energy ends up in the useful form. No device is 100% efficient.

**Supplement:** radiation from the Sun is the main source of energy for all our resources except geothermal, nuclear and tidal. (Fossil fuels and biofuels stored the Sun's energy through photosynthesis; the Sun's heating drives wind, waves and the water cycle that fills dams.) The Sun releases energy by nuclear fusion, and research is being carried out into fusion power stations that could generate electrical energy on a large scale.

### Power — 1.7.4

Power is the work done per unit time, or the energy transferred per unit time. Unit: watt (W); 1 W = 1 J/s. A 2 kW kettle transfers 2000 J every second.

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| W = Fd = ΔE | W: work done, J; F: force, N; d: distance moved in the direction of the force, m; ΔE: energy transferred, J | Any force moving an object; lifting, pushing, braking. | Core |
| P = W ÷ t | P: power, W; W: work done, J; t: time, s | Power from work done. Rearranged: W = Pt. | Core |
| P = ΔE ÷ t | ΔE: energy transferred, J | Power from energy transferred (heaters, lamps, motors). | Core |
| Eₖ = ½mv² | Eₖ: kinetic energy, J; m: mass, kg; v: speed, m/s | Energy of a moving object. Rearranged: v = √(2Eₖ ÷ m). | Supplement |
| ΔEₚ = mgΔh | ΔEₚ: change in gravitational potential energy, J; m: kg; g: 9.8 N/kg; Δh: change in height, m | Raising or lowering an object near the Earth's surface. | Supplement |
| % efficiency = (useful energy output ÷ total energy input) × 100% | Both energies in J | Energy form of efficiency. | Supplement |
| % efficiency = (useful power output ÷ total power input) × 100% | Both powers in W | Power form of efficiency. | Supplement |

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: work done by a force

**Example — pushing a box.** A 60 N force pushes a box 4.0 m along the floor. W = 60 × 4.0 = 240 J.

**Example — lifting.** Lifting a 50 N bag onto a shelf 1.6 m high: the force needed equals the weight, so W = 50 × 1.6 = 80 J.

**Example — braking distance.** A car's brakes exert 6000 N and do 180 000 J of work to stop it. d = W ÷ F = 180 000 ÷ 6000 = 30 m.

### Situation: power

**Example — motor.** A motor lifts a 300 N load through 12 m in 20 s.

- W = 300 × 12 = 3600 J.
- P = 3600 ÷ 20 = 180 W.

**Example — energy used.** A 1500 W hair dryer runs for 5 minutes. E = Pt = 1500 × 300 = 450 000 J = 450 kJ.

**Example — running upstairs.** A 600 N student climbs 3.0 m of stairs in 4.0 s. Power = (600 × 3.0) ÷ 4.0 = 450 W.

### Situation: kinetic energy (Supplement)

**Example — car.** A 900 kg car moves at 20 m/s. Eₖ = ½ × 900 × 20² = 180 000 J.

**Example — doubling the speed.** At 40 m/s the same car has ½ × 900 × 40² = 720 000 J: four times as much.

**Example — find the speed.** A 0.50 kg ball has 64 J of kinetic energy. v² = 2 × 64 ÷ 0.50 = 256, so v = 16 m/s.

### Situation: gravitational potential energy (Supplement)

**Example — lifting a box.** A 15 kg box is lifted 2.0 m. ΔEₚ = 15 × 9.8 × 2.0 = 294 J.

### Situation: falling and swinging, energy conservation (Supplement)

**Example — dropped object.** A ball is dropped from 5.0 m. Ignoring air resistance, what is its speed when it hits the ground?

- Loss of Eₚ = gain of Eₖ, so mgΔh = ½mv². The mass cancels.
- v² = 2gΔh = 2 × 9.8 × 5.0 = 98, so v = 9.9 m/s.

**Example — pendulum or roller coaster.** A roller coaster car starts at rest 20 m above the lowest point. Ignoring friction: v² = 2 × 9.8 × 20 = 392, so v = 19.8 m/s at the bottom.

**Example — how high does it go?** A ball is thrown straight up at 12 m/s. At the top, all the kinetic energy has become potential energy: Δh = v² ÷ 2g = 144 ÷ 19.6 = 7.3 m.

**Example — with friction.** A 40 kg child slides down a slide 3.0 m high and reaches the bottom at 6.0 m/s.

- Eₚ lost = 40 × 9.8 × 3.0 = 1176 J.
- Eₖ gained = ½ × 40 × 6.0² = 720 J.
- Energy transferred to internal energy by friction = 1176 − 720 = 456 J.

### Situation: efficiency (Supplement)

**Example — lamp.** A lamp is supplied with 200 J and gives out 30 J of light. Efficiency = (30 ÷ 200) × 100% = 15%. The other 170 J heats the surroundings.

**Example — motor.** A motor has an input power of 800 W and an efficiency of 75%. Useful output power = 0.75 × 800 = 600 W.

**Example — find the input.** A power station delivers 400 MW of electrical power at 40% efficiency. Input power = 400 ÷ 0.40 = 1000 MW, so 600 MW is wasted, mostly heating the surroundings.

**Example — Sankey diagram.** An arrow for 500 J input splits into 350 J useful and 150 J wasted. Efficiency = 350 ÷ 500 × 100% = 70%. In a scale drawing, the 350 J arrow is 70% of the width of the input arrow.

## Exam checks

- Name the energy stores precisely: "gravitational potential", "kinetic", "internal (thermal)"; avoid "movement energy" and "heat energy".
- Work uses the distance moved in the direction of the force.
- Convert to J, s, kg and m/s first: kJ × 1000, minutes × 60, g ÷ 1000.
- In Eₖ = ½mv² only the speed is squared. Square it before multiplying.
- In energy-conservation questions, mass often cancels; say which energy store loses and which gains.
- Efficiency can never be more than 100%, and it has no unit.
- For an energy resource, compare it on renewability, availability, reliability, scale and environmental impact.
