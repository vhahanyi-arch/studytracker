# Unit 13 — Electromagnetic spectrum and sound

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 3.3 and 3.4, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### The electromagnetic spectrum — 3.3

Electromagnetic waves are transverse waves that all travel at the same high speed in a vacuum. **Supplement:** that speed is 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s, and it is approximately the same in air.

In order of increasing frequency (decreasing wavelength):

| Region | Typical uses | Harmful effects of excessive exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Radio waves (longest wavelength, lowest frequency) | Radio and television transmissions, astronomy, radio frequency identification (RFID) tags | — |
| Microwaves | Satellite television, mobile phones, microwave ovens | Internal heating of body cells |
| Infrared | Electric grills, remote controls for TVs, intruder alarms, thermal imaging, optical fibres | Skin burns |
| Visible light | Vision, photography, illumination | — |
| Ultraviolet | Security marking (seen under UV lamps), detecting fake bank notes, sterilising water | Damage to surface cells and eyes, leading to skin cancer and eye conditions |
| X-rays | Medical scanning (bones), security scanners at airports | Mutation or damage to cells in the body |
| Gamma rays (shortest wavelength, highest frequency) | Sterilising food and medical equipment, detecting and treating cancer | Mutation or damage to cells in the body |

A memory aid for the order: Radio, Micro, Infrared, Visible, Ultraviolet, X, Gamma. Higher frequency means more energy carried and generally more danger.

### Communication with satellites — 3.3

Communication with artificial satellites is mainly by microwaves, because they pass through the atmosphere.

- Some satellite phones use satellites in low orbits (close to Earth, so a weaker signal is needed, but each satellite moves across the sky, so many are needed).
- Some satellite phones and direct-broadcast satellite TV use geostationary satellites, which stay above the same point on the equator, so a dish can point at one fixed position.

### Communication systems — 3.3 (Supplement)

- **Mobile phones and wireless internet** use microwaves: they can pass through some walls and need only a short aerial to send and receive.
- **Bluetooth** uses radio waves: they pass through walls, though the signal is weakened.
- **Optical fibres** (visible light or short-wavelength infrared) carry cable TV and high-speed broadband: glass is transparent to these wavelengths, and their high frequency can carry data at high rates.

### Analogue and digital signals — 3.3 (Supplement)

An analogue signal varies continuously and can take any value. A digital signal has only two values, on and off (1 and 0). Sound can be sent either way: a microphone produces an analogue signal, which can be converted to digital.

Benefits of digital signals:

- a higher rate of data transmission;
- a longer range, because at each regenerator (amplifier) along the way the signal can be cleaned up exactly: noise picked up on the way is removed, since each pulse only needs to be recognised as a 1 or a 0. Amplifying an analogue signal amplifies its noise too.

### Sound — 3.4

Sound is produced by vibrating sources (a guitar string, a loudspeaker cone, vocal cords). The vibrations pass through a medium as a longitudinal wave: the particles vibrate backwards and forwards along the direction the wave travels.

Sound needs a medium (solid, liquid or gas); it cannot travel through a vacuum. Shown by the bell-in-a-jar experiment: as air is pumped out, the ringing fades, though you can still see the hammer striking.

- Humans hear frequencies from about 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz.
- Ultrasound is sound with a frequency higher than 20 kHz (20 000 Hz).
- The speed of sound in air is about 330 to 350 m/s. **Supplement:** sound generally travels faster in solids than in liquids, and faster in liquids than in gases (about 1500 m/s in water, about 5000 m/s in steel).
- An echo is the reflection of a sound wave off a hard surface.

Loudness depends on amplitude: a larger amplitude sounds louder. Pitch depends on frequency: a higher frequency sounds higher.

**Supplement — compressions and rarefactions:** a compression is a region where the particles are closer together (higher pressure); a rarefaction is a region where they are further apart (lower pressure). The wavelength is the distance from one compression to the next.

### Measuring the speed of sound in air — 3.4

**Echo method:** stand a measured distance (for example 100 m) from a large flat wall. Clap and adjust your clapping rate until each clap coincides with the echo of the one before. Time 20 claps; the sound travels to the wall and back (2 × 100 m) in each clap interval. Speed = 2 × distance ÷ time for one interval.

**Two-observer method:** one person fires a starting pistol (or bangs cymbals) a measured distance away (several hundred metres). The other starts a stopwatch on seeing the flash (light arrives almost instantly) and stops it on hearing the bang. Speed = distance ÷ time. Repeat and average; swap positions to cancel the effect of wind.

### Uses of ultrasound — 3.4 (Supplement)

Ultrasound pulses reflect from boundaries between different materials. The time for the echo to return gives the distance.

- **Non-destructive testing:** detecting cracks inside metal castings or welds; an echo returns early from a crack.
- **Medical scanning of soft tissue:** imaging a fetus or organs; it is safe because it is not ionising.
- **Sonar:** finding the depth of the sea or locating shoals of fish and submarines.

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| v = fλ | v: speed, m/s; f: frequency, Hz; λ: wavelength, m | Any electromagnetic or sound wave. | Core |
| v = d ÷ t | d: distance travelled by the sound, m; t: time, s | Speed of sound by the two-observer method; any wave over a known distance. | Core |
| distance to reflector = (v × t) ÷ 2 | t: time from sending to receiving the echo, s | Echoes, sonar and ultrasound: the sound goes there and back. | Supplement |
| speed of EM waves in a vacuum = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s | m/s | All EM wave calculations; the same value in air. | Supplement |

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: electromagnetic waves

**Example — microwaves.** A microwave oven uses 2.45 GHz. λ = 3.0 × 10⁸ ÷ (2.45 × 10⁹) = 0.12 m.

**Example — X-rays.** An X-ray has wavelength 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. f = 3.0 × 10⁸ ÷ (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 3.0 × 10¹⁸ Hz.

### Situation: sound wavelength and frequency

**Example — find the wavelength.** A 256 Hz tuning fork in air (340 m/s): λ = 340 ÷ 256 = 1.3 m.

**Example — ultrasound.** 40 kHz ultrasound in air (340 m/s): λ = 340 ÷ 40 000 = 0.0085 m = 8.5 mm.

### Situation: two-observer speed of sound

**Example.** A starting pistol is fired 660 m away; the bang is heard 2.0 s after the flash is seen. v = 660 ÷ 2.0 = 330 m/s.

### Situation: echo off a wall

**Example — find the speed.** A student 85 m from a wall hears the echo of a clap 0.50 s later. The sound travels 2 × 85 = 170 m. v = 170 ÷ 0.50 = 340 m/s.

**Example — find the distance.** An echo returns 1.2 s after a shout; v = 340 m/s. Distance to the cliff = (340 × 1.2) ÷ 2 = 204 m.

### Situation: sonar and ultrasound (Supplement)

**Example — depth of the sea.** A ship's sonar pulse returns after 0.80 s; sound travels at 1500 m/s in sea water. Depth = (1500 × 0.80) ÷ 2 = 600 m.

**Example — crack in metal.** Ultrasound in steel (5000 m/s). The echo from the far side of a block returns after 40 µs, but another echo returns after 16 µs.

- Thickness = (5000 × 40 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ 2 = 0.10 m.
- Crack depth = (5000 × 16 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ 2 = 0.040 m = 4.0 cm below the surface.

### Situation: thunder and lightning

**Example.** Thunder is heard 3.0 s after the lightning flash; v = 340 m/s. Distance = 340 × 3.0 = 1020 m, about 1 km. (No halving: the sound only travels one way.)

### Situation: comparing sounds

**Example — reading an oscilloscope.** Trace B has taller peaks than trace A but the same spacing: B is louder, same pitch. Trace C has peaks closer together than A: C has a higher frequency, so a higher pitch.

## Exam checks

- Learn the EM spectrum order both ways, and one use and one hazard for each region.
- Sound cannot travel through a vacuum; light can.
- In echo questions, halve the total distance (there and back) — but not in one-way questions such as thunder.
- Loudness ↔ amplitude; pitch ↔ frequency.
- Ultrasound is above 20 kHz; convert kHz to Hz before calculating.
- Speeds: light 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s; sound in air about 330 to 350 m/s; sound is faster in liquids and faster still in solids.
