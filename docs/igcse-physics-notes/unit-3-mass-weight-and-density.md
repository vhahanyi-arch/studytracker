# Unit 3 — Mass, weight and density

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 1.3 and 1.4, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Mass and weight — 1.3

Mass is a measure of the quantity of matter in an object at rest relative to the observer. It is measured in kilograms and does not change when the object moves to the Moon.

Weight is a gravitational force on an object that has mass. It is a force, so it is measured in newtons and has a direction (towards the centre of the planet). Weight depends on where you are: an astronaut of mass 70 kg weighs about 690 N on Earth but only about 110 N on the Moon, where g is about 1.6 N/kg.

Gravitational field strength g is the force per unit mass. On Earth it is about 9.8 N/kg. The number is the same as the acceleration of free fall in m/s², because N/kg and m/s² are equivalent units.

A balance compares weights. Since the two objects are in the same gravitational field, equal weights also means equal masses, so a balance compares masses too.

**Supplement:** weight is the effect of a gravitational field on a mass. Wherever there is a gravitational field, a mass in it has weight; change the field strength and the weight changes while the mass stays the same.

### Density — 1.4

Density is mass per unit volume. It tells you how tightly packed the matter in a material is. Water has a density of 1000 kg/m³, which is 1.0 g/cm³. A material does not change its density if you cut it up: a small piece of steel has the same density as a large block.

### Measuring density — 1.4

Find the mass on a balance and the volume by one of these methods, then divide.

- Regular solid (cuboid, cylinder, sphere): measure its dimensions with a ruler or calipers and calculate the volume. Cuboid: length × width × height. Cylinder: π r² × length.
- Irregular solid that sinks: displacement. Part-fill a measuring cylinder with water, read the volume, lower the object in on a thread, read again. The volume of the object is the rise in the reading. (A displacement can with a spout into a measuring cylinder works for larger objects.)
- Liquid: weigh an empty measuring cylinder, pour in a known volume of liquid, weigh again. The mass of liquid is the difference.

### Floating and sinking — 1.4

An object floats in a liquid if its density is less than the liquid's density, and sinks if its density is greater. Ice (about 920 kg/m³) floats on water (1000 kg/m³); steel (about 7800 kg/m³) sinks.

**Supplement:** two liquids that do not mix form layers, with the less dense liquid floating on top. Oil (about 900 kg/m³) floats on water.

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| g = W ÷ m | g: gravitational field strength, N/kg; W: weight, N; m: mass, kg | Field strength, or weight from mass: W = mg. Mass from weight: m = W ÷ g. | Core |
| ρ = m ÷ V | ρ (rho): density, kg/m³ or g/cm³; m: mass, kg or g; V: volume, m³ or cm³ | Any density question. Rearranged: m = ρV, V = m ÷ ρ. Keep the units matched (g with cm³, kg with m³). | Core |
| V (cuboid) = l × w × h | lengths in m or cm | Regular solid. | Core |
| V (displaced) = reading after − reading before | cm³ (= ml) | Irregular solid that sinks. | Core |

Unit conversion: 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³. To turn g/cm³ into kg/m³, multiply by 1000.

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: weight from mass, and the other way round

**Example — weight on Earth.** A bag of mass 12 kg. W = mg = 12 × 9.8 = 118 N (to 3 s.f.).

**Example — mass from a weight.** A newton meter reads 4.9 N. m = W ÷ g = 4.9 ÷ 9.8 = 0.50 kg.

### Situation: a different planet

**Example — Moon.** A rover of mass 180 kg is taken to the Moon, where g = 1.6 N/kg.

- Its mass is still 180 kg.
- Its weight on the Moon = 180 × 1.6 = 288 N, compared with 180 × 9.8 = 1764 N on Earth.

**Example — finding g.** A 5.0 kg mass weighs 18.5 N on Mars. g = 18.5 ÷ 5.0 = 3.7 N/kg.

### Situation: density of a regular solid

**Example — metal block.** A block measures 2.0 cm × 3.0 cm × 5.0 cm and has a mass of 81 g.

- V = 2.0 × 3.0 × 5.0 = 30 cm³.
- ρ = 81 ÷ 30 = 2.7 g/cm³ = 2700 kg/m³ (aluminium).

### Situation: density of an irregular solid (displacement)

**Example — a stone.** Water in a measuring cylinder reads 40 cm³; with the stone in it reads 65 cm³. The stone's mass is 70 g.

- V = 65 − 40 = 25 cm³.
- ρ = 70 ÷ 25 = 2.8 g/cm³.

### Situation: density of a liquid

**Example — cooking oil.** Empty cylinder 120 g; with 50 cm³ of oil 166 g.

- Mass of oil = 166 − 120 = 46 g.
- ρ = 46 ÷ 50 = 0.92 g/cm³ = 920 kg/m³.

### Situation: mass or volume from density

**Example — mass of air in a room.** A room is 5.0 m × 4.0 m × 2.5 m; air has density 1.2 kg/m³.

- V = 5.0 × 4.0 × 2.5 = 50 m³.
- m = ρV = 1.2 × 50 = 60 kg.

**Example — volume of a gold bar.** A gold bar has mass 12.5 kg; gold has density 19 300 kg/m³. V = m ÷ ρ = 12.5 ÷ 19 300 = 6.5 × 10⁻⁴ m³ (650 cm³).

### Situation: will it float?

**Example — comparing densities.** A wooden block has mass 300 g and volume 400 cm³.

- ρ = 300 ÷ 400 = 0.75 g/cm³, which is less than water's 1.0 g/cm³, so it floats.
- (Supplement) In a column of syrup (1.4 g/cm³), water (1.0 g/cm³) and oil (0.9 g/cm³), the liquids settle in that order from the bottom: syrup, water, oil. The block (0.75 g/cm³) floats on top of the oil.

## Exam checks

- Mass in kg, weight in N. Never give weight in kg.
- Mass does not change from place to place; weight does, because g changes.
- Match density units: g and cm³ give g/cm³; kg and m³ give kg/m³.
- For displacement, the volume is the difference between the two readings, not the final reading.
- To decide floating, compare densities, not masses: a huge ship floats and a small stone sinks.
