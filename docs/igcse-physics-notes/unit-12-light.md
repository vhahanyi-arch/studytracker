# Unit 12 — Light

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 3.2.1, 3.2.2, 3.2.3 and 3.2.4, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Reflection — 3.2.1

The normal is a line drawn at 90° to a surface at the point where a ray hits it. The angle of incidence, i, is between the incident ray and the normal; the angle of reflection, r, is between the reflected ray and the normal. Angles are always measured from the normal, not from the surface.

Law of reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.

The image in a plane mirror is:

- the same size as the object;
- the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front;
- virtual (it cannot be projected onto a screen; the rays only appear to come from it);
- upright, and laterally inverted (left and right swapped).

**Supplement — constructions:** to locate the image, draw a line from the object at 90° to the mirror and continue it the same distance behind. To draw a ray reaching an eye, join the image to the eye with a straight line; where it crosses the mirror is where the real ray reflects; draw the ray from the object to that point, then on to the eye. Use solid lines for real rays and dashed lines behind the mirror.

### Refraction — 3.2.2

Refraction is the change in direction of light as it crosses a boundary between two media at an angle, because its speed changes. Light slows down in glass or water compared with air.

- Entering a denser medium (air → glass): light slows and bends towards the normal.
- Leaving a denser medium (glass → air): light speeds up and bends away from the normal.
- A ray along the normal (i = 0) does not change direction, though its speed still changes.

**Experiment:** place a rectangular glass block on paper and draw round it. Shine a ray from a ray box into one side at an angle; mark the ray with crosses where it enters and leaves. Remove the block and join the points to show the path inside. Measure i and r from the normal. Repeat for different angles and with semicircular or triangular blocks. A rectangular block gives an emerging ray parallel to the incident ray, but shifted sideways.

### Critical angle and total internal reflection — 3.2.2

When light travels from a denser medium towards a less dense one (glass → air), some is reflected internally and some refracts out. As the angle of incidence increases, the refracted ray bends closer to the surface. The critical angle, c, is the angle of incidence for which the angle of refraction is 90°.

If the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle, all the light is reflected back inside: total internal reflection. Conditions: light must travel from the denser medium to the less dense one, and i must be greater than c. For glass c is about 42°.

Examples: a semicircular block with a ray aimed at its centre (increase the angle until the ray reflects totally); periscopes and binoculars using 45° prisms; the sparkle of a cut diamond; optical fibres.

**Supplement — refractive index:** n is the ratio of the speed of light in two regions: the speed in air (or a vacuum) divided by the speed in the material. Glass has n ≈ 1.5, so light travels 1.5 times slower in glass. A larger n means more bending.

**Supplement — optical fibres:** thin, flexible glass fibres. Light entering one end hits the inside wall at more than the critical angle and is totally internally reflected again and again along the fibre, even round bends. Uses: telecommunications (telephone, cable TV and broadband, carrying data as pulses of light or infrared at high rates over long distances with little loss) and endoscopes for seeing inside the body.

### Thin lenses — 3.2.3

A converging (convex) lens is thicker in the middle and makes a parallel beam of light meet at a point: the principal focus (focal point). A diverging (concave) lens is thinner in the middle and makes a parallel beam spread out, as if from a principal focus behind the lens.

- **Principal axis:** the line through the centre of the lens at 90° to it.
- **Focal length, f:** the distance from the centre of the lens to the principal focus. A fatter (more curved) lens has a shorter focal length.

**Ray diagram for a converging lens (real image):** use two of these three rays from the top of the object.

- A ray parallel to the principal axis refracts through the principal focus on the far side.
- A ray through the centre of the lens goes straight on.
- A ray through the principal focus on the near side emerges parallel to the axis.

Where the rays cross is the top of the image. A real image is formed where rays actually meet; it can be shown on a screen.

| Object position | Image |
|---|---|
| Further than 2f | Real, inverted, diminished, between f and 2f on the other side (camera, eye) |
| At 2f | Real, inverted, same size, at 2f |
| Between f and 2f | Real, inverted, enlarged, beyond 2f (projector) |
| At f | No image: the rays emerge parallel |
| Closer than f (Supplement) | Virtual, upright, enlarged, on the same side as the object (magnifying glass) |

Describe an image with three terms: enlarged / same size / diminished; upright / inverted; real / virtual.

A virtual image forms where diverging rays appear to come from when traced backwards (drawn dashed). It cannot be projected onto a screen.

**Supplement — virtual image ray diagram:** with the object closer than f, the two rays leave the lens diverging. Extend them backwards with dashed lines; they meet on the object's side, giving an upright, enlarged, virtual image. This is a magnifying glass: hold a converging lens so the object is inside its focal length and look through the lens.

**Supplement — correcting sight:**

- **Short sight:** distant objects are blurred because the eye focuses their light in front of the retina (the eye lens is too strong or the eyeball too long). Corrected with a diverging lens, which spreads the rays slightly before they enter the eye.
- **Long sight:** near objects are blurred because the light would focus behind the retina (the eye lens is too weak). Corrected with a converging lens, which starts converging the rays before they enter the eye.

### Dispersion — 3.2.4

White light passing through a glass prism splits into a spectrum of colours: dispersion. Each colour has a different frequency and slows by a different amount in glass, so each is refracted by a different angle. Violet is refracted most, red least.

The seven colours in order of increasing wavelength (decreasing frequency): violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red. Red has the longest wavelength and lowest frequency; violet has the shortest wavelength and highest frequency.

**Supplement:** light of a single frequency is monochromatic (a single colour); a laser gives monochromatic light.

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| i = r (reflection) | i: angle of incidence; r: angle of reflection; both from the normal, degrees | Any plane-mirror question. | Core |
| image distance behind mirror = object distance in front | m or cm | Locating plane-mirror images. | Core |
| n = sin i ÷ sin r | n: refractive index, no unit; i: angle in air; r: angle in the material | Light passing between air and a material. Rearranged: sin r = sin i ÷ n. | Supplement |
| n = speed in air ÷ speed in material | speeds in m/s | The definition of n; speed of light in air ≈ 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s. | Supplement |
| n = 1 ÷ sin c | c: critical angle, degrees | Total internal reflection. Rearranged: sin c = 1 ÷ n. | Supplement |

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: plane mirror

**Example — angle between rays.** A ray hits a mirror at 30° to the surface. Its angle of incidence is 90 − 30 = 60°, so the angle of reflection is 60°. The angle between the incident and reflected rays is 120°.

**Example — image position.** A person stands 1.5 m in front of a mirror. The image is 1.5 m behind the mirror, so 3.0 m from the person. If they step 0.5 m closer, the image is 2.0 m from them.

### Situation: find the refractive index (Supplement)

**Example — glass block.** A ray enters glass with i = 40° and r = 25°. n = sin 40° ÷ sin 25° = 0.643 ÷ 0.423 = 1.52.

### Situation: find the angle of refraction (Supplement)

**Example — water.** Light enters water (n = 1.33) at i = 50°. sin r = sin 50° ÷ 1.33 = 0.766 ÷ 1.33 = 0.576, so r = 35°.

### Situation: find the speed in the material (Supplement)

**Example — diamond.** n = 2.4. Speed in diamond = 3.0 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.4 = 1.25 × 10⁸ m/s.

### Situation: critical angle (Supplement)

**Example — find c.** For glass with n = 1.5: sin c = 1 ÷ 1.5 = 0.667, so c = 42°.

**Example — find n.** A block has a critical angle of 37°. n = 1 ÷ sin 37° = 1 ÷ 0.602 = 1.66.

**Example — does it reflect totally?** Light inside glass (c = 42°) meets the surface at 45°. Since 45° is greater than 42° and the light is going from glass to air, it is totally internally reflected. At 30° it would mostly refract out into the air.

### Situation: lens images

**Example — camera.** An object is 30 cm from a converging lens of focal length 10 cm. It is beyond 2f (20 cm), so the image is real, inverted and diminished, between 10 cm and 20 cm on the other side (a scale drawing gives 15 cm).

**Example — magnifying glass (Supplement).** An object 4 cm from a lens of focal length 6 cm is inside f, so the image is virtual, upright and enlarged, on the same side as the object.

## Exam checks

- Measure every angle from the normal.
- Plane-mirror images are virtual, upright, the same size and the same distance behind the mirror.
- Towards the normal when slowing down (into glass), away from the normal when speeding up (into air).
- Total internal reflection needs both conditions: dense to less dense, and i greater than c.
- In n = sin i ÷ sin r, i is the angle in air. Use your calculator in degree mode.
- Draw rays with arrows and a ruler; virtual rays dashed.
- Spectrum order by wavelength: violet (shortest) to red (longest).
