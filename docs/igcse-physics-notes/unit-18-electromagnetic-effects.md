# Unit 18 — Electromagnetic effects

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 4.5.1, 4.5.2, 4.5.3, 4.5.4, 4.5.5 and 4.5.6, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Electromagnetic induction — 4.5.1

An e.m.f. is induced in a conductor when it moves across a magnetic field (cutting field lines), or when the magnetic field linking it changes. If the conductor is part of a complete circuit, the induced e.m.f. drives a current.

**Experiment:** connect a coil to a sensitive centre-zero meter (galvanometer). Push a bar magnet into the coil: the needle flicks one way. Hold it still inside: no reading. Pull it out: the needle flicks the other way. Moving the coil instead of the magnet has the same effect. A straight wire moved up and down between the poles of a magnet also gives a reading.

The size of the induced e.m.f. increases with:

- the speed of the movement (the rate at which field lines are cut or the field changes);
- the strength of the magnetic field;
- the number of turns on the coil.

**Supplement:** the direction of the induced e.m.f. opposes the change causing it. Push the N pole of a magnet into a coil and the induced current makes the near end of the coil an N pole, which repels the magnet. Pull it out and that end becomes an S pole, attracting the magnet back. You must do work against this opposing force, and that work is the energy that becomes electrical energy.

**Supplement — relative directions (Fleming's right-hand rule):** for a wire moved through a magnetic field, hold the thumb, first finger and second finger of the right hand at right angles. Thumb = motion (direction of the force moving the wire), First finger = Field (N to S), seCond finger = induced Current (conventional).

### The a.c. generator — 4.5.2 (Supplement)

A coil of wire rotates in a magnetic field (or a magnet rotates inside a coil). As the coil turns, its sides cut the field lines, inducing an alternating e.m.f. Each end of the coil is connected to its own slip ring, which turns with the coil; carbon brushes press against the slip rings to take the current to the external circuit without the wires twisting.

**Graph of e.m.f. against time:** a smooth wave (sinusoidal), positive then negative each turn.

- **Peak e.m.f. (positive or negative):** when the plane of the coil is parallel to the magnetic field lines. Its sides are moving at right angles to the field, cutting the lines fastest.
- **Zero e.m.f.:** when the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field lines. Its sides are moving along the field lines, not cutting them.
- One complete rotation gives one complete cycle: a peak, zero, a trough, zero.

Turning faster gives a larger peak e.m.f. and more cycles per second (higher frequency). A stronger magnet or more turns gives a larger peak at the same frequency.

### Magnetic effect of a current — 4.5.3

A current in a wire produces a magnetic field around it.

- **Straight wire:** the field lines are concentric circles around the wire, in planes at right angles to it. Direction by the right-hand grip rule: grip the wire with the thumb pointing along the conventional current; the fingers curl in the direction of the field.
- **Solenoid (coil):** the field outside is like that of a bar magnet; inside, the lines are straight, parallel and close together (a strong, uniform field). Grip the coil with the fingers curling in the direction of the current; the thumb points to the N pole.

**Experiment:** pass a vertical wire through a horizontal card. Sprinkle iron filings on the card and tap it while a large current flows: the filings form circles. Place plotting compasses on the card to show the direction; reverse the current and the compasses reverse. Do the same with a solenoid threaded through a card.

**Supplement:** the field around a straight wire is strongest close to the wire and gets weaker further away (the circles are drawn further apart). The field of a solenoid is strongest inside. Increasing the current makes either field stronger; reversing the current reverses the field direction. For a solenoid, more turns (and a soft-iron core) also make the field stronger.

**Uses:**

- **Relay:** a small current through a coil magnetises a soft-iron core, which attracts an iron armature and closes a pair of contacts in a second circuit. A small, safe current can then switch a large current, for example a car starter motor or a high-power heater controlled by a thermostat.
- **Loudspeaker:** an alternating current from an amplifier flows in a coil attached to a paper cone and placed in the field of a permanent magnet. The force on the coil reverses as the current reverses, so the cone vibrates at the frequency of the current, producing sound.
- **Electric bell, electromagnetic lock, scrap-yard crane.**

### Force on a current-carrying conductor — 4.5.4

A wire carrying a current in a magnetic field experiences a force (the motor effect). The force is largest when the wire is at right angles to the field, and zero when the wire is parallel to the field.

**Experiment:** place a loose wire (or a rolling rod on two rails) between the poles of a horseshoe magnet and pass a current: the wire jumps (or rolls). Reverse the current, and the force reverses. Reverse the field (turn the magnet round), and the force reverses. Reverse both, and the force is in the original direction.

**Supplement — relative directions (Fleming's left-hand rule):** hold the thumb, first finger and second finger of the left hand at right angles. First finger = Field (N to S), seCond finger = conventional Current, thuMb = Motion (force).

**Supplement — beams of charged particles:** a beam of moving charged particles is a current, so it is deflected by a magnetic field in the same way. For a beam of positive particles, the conventional current is in the direction they travel. For electrons, the conventional current is opposite to their direction of travel, so point the second finger backwards along the beam.

### The d.c. motor — 4.5.5

A current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences a turning effect: the forces on its two sides are in opposite directions (one up, one down), producing a moment. The turning effect increases with:

- more turns on the coil;
- a larger current;
- a stronger magnetic field.

**Supplement — operation:** a split-ring commutator connects the coil to the d.c. supply through two brushes. Every half-turn, just as the coil passes the vertical position, the split ring swaps the coil's connections, reversing the current in the coil. The forces then keep turning the coil in the same direction. Without the commutator the coil would rock back and forth and stop. The coil's momentum carries it past the vertical, where the turning effect is momentarily zero.

### The transformer — 4.5.6

A transformer is two coils of insulated wire, the primary and the secondary, wound on the same soft-iron core.

- **Step-up transformer:** more turns on the secondary than the primary; the output p.d. is greater than the input.
- **Step-down transformer:** fewer turns on the secondary; the output p.d. is smaller.

**High-voltage transmission:** at the power station, a step-up transformer raises the p.d. to hundreds of thousands of volts for the transmission cables; near homes, step-down transformers reduce it to the mains p.d. Advantages of high-voltage transmission: for the same power, the current is much smaller, so less energy is wasted heating the cables, and thinner, cheaper cables (with fewer pylons) can be used.

**Supplement — principle:** an alternating current in the primary coil produces an alternating magnetic field in the soft-iron core. This changing field passes through (links) the secondary coil and induces an alternating e.m.f. in it. A transformer only works with a.c.: a steady d.c. produces a steady field, which induces nothing. Soft iron is used because it is easily magnetised and demagnetised as the field reverses.

**Supplement:** in an ideal (100% efficient) transformer the power in equals the power out, so stepping up the p.d. steps down the current by the same factor. Power lost in cables is P = I²R: halving the current reduces the loss to a quarter.

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| V_p ÷ V_s = N_p ÷ N_s | V_p, V_s: primary and secondary p.d., V; N_p, N_s: number of turns on each coil | Any transformer. Rearranged: V_s = V_p × N_s ÷ N_p. | Core |
| I_p × V_p = I_s × V_s | I_p, I_s: primary and secondary current, A | 100% efficient transformer: power in = power out. | Supplement |
| P = I²R | P: power lost as heating, W; I: current in cable, A; R: cable resistance, Ω | Energy wasted in transmission cables. | Supplement |
| P = IV | W | Power transmitted (from Unit 15); gives the current for a given power and p.d. | Core |

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: find the output p.d.

**Example — step-down.** A transformer has 2000 turns on the primary and 100 on the secondary; the input is 230 V a.c. V_s = 230 × 100 ÷ 2000 = 11.5 V.

**Example — step-up.** 400 primary turns, 20 000 secondary turns, input 25 000 V. V_s = 25 000 × 20 000 ÷ 400 = 1 250 000 V. (Real grid transformers step up to about 400 000 V.)

### Situation: find the number of turns

**Example — phone charger.** A charger steps 230 V down to 5.75 V using a primary of 4000 turns. N_s = N_p × V_s ÷ V_p = 4000 × 5.75 ÷ 230 = 100 turns.

### Situation: currents in an ideal transformer (Supplement)

**Example — find the primary current.** A 100% efficient transformer gives 12 V and 5.0 A to a lamp from a 240 V supply.

- Power out = 12 × 5.0 = 60 W = power in.
- I_p = 60 ÷ 240 = 0.25 A.

### Situation: power loss in cables (Supplement)

**Example — compare two p.d.s.** A power station sends 1.0 MW through cables of total resistance 5.0 Ω.

- At 10 000 V: I = P ÷ V = 1 000 000 ÷ 10 000 = 100 A. Loss = I²R = 100² × 5.0 = 50 000 W (5% of the power).
- At 100 000 V: I = 10 A. Loss = 10² × 5.0 = 500 W (0.05%).
- Ten times the p.d. gives one tenth of the current and one hundredth of the power loss.

### Situation: predict a direction (Supplement)

**Example — motor effect.** A wire runs from left to right through a field pointing into the page, with conventional current left to right. Left hand: first finger into the page, second finger to the right; the thumb points up. The force on the wire is upwards.

**Example — electron beam.** Electrons move left to right through the same field. The conventional current is right to left, so the force is downwards.

**Example — generator.** A wire is pushed upward through a field pointing into the page. Right hand: thumb up, first finger into the page; the second finger points left, so the induced current flows from right to left.

### Situation: reading a generator graph (Supplement)

**Example.** The e.m.f. peaks at 4 V every 0.10 s (one peak per cycle). The frequency is 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10 Hz, so the coil turns 10 times a second. Doubling the rotation speed doubles the frequency to 20 Hz and roughly doubles the peak to 8 V.

## Exam checks

- Induction needs relative movement or a changing field. A magnet held still in a coil induces nothing.
- Factors for a larger induced e.m.f.: faster motion, stronger field, more turns.
- Left hand for motors (force), right hand for generators (induced current). Use conventional current.
- Slip rings: a.c. generator. Split-ring commutator: d.c. motor.
- Transformers need a.c. and a soft-iron core. Step-up raises p.d. and lowers current.
- Explain high-voltage transmission: lower current, so less I²R heating in the cables.
