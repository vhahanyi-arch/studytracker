# Unit 9 — Thermal properties and temperature

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 2.2.1, 2.2.2 and 2.2.3, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Thermal expansion — 2.2.1

Most materials expand when heated (at constant pressure) and contract when cooled. For the same temperature rise, gases expand most, liquids less, and solids least.

Everyday applications and consequences:

- Gaps are left between sections of railway track and in bridges (expansion joints) so they do not buckle in hot weather.
- Overhead power cables are hung with some slack so they do not snap when they contract in cold weather.
- A liquid-in-glass thermometer works because the liquid expands up the narrow tube as it warms.
- A bimetallic strip (two metals that expand by different amounts) bends when heated; it is used in thermostats and fire alarms.
- A tight metal lid on a glass jar can be loosened by running it under hot water.

**Supplement:** in a solid the particles are held tightly by strong forces, so extra vibration pushes them only slightly further apart: small expansion. In a liquid the forces are weaker, so the particles move further apart: more expansion. In a gas the forces are almost zero, so the particles spread out freely: by far the largest expansion.

### Internal energy and specific heat capacity — 2.2.2

A rise in the temperature of an object increases its internal energy.

**Supplement:** a temperature rise means an increase in the average kinetic energy of all the particles in the object.

**Supplement:** specific heat capacity, c, is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C. Water has a high value (4200 J/(kg °C)), so it takes a lot of energy to heat and stores a lot; that is why it is used in central heating and car cooling systems. Metals have low values (copper about 390 J/(kg °C)), so they heat up quickly.

**Supplement — measuring c of a solid:** use a metal block with two holes, one for an electric immersion heater and one for a thermometer (a drop of oil helps contact). Measure the block's mass. Record the starting temperature, switch on the heater, and use a joulemeter (or a voltmeter, ammeter and stopwatch: E = IVt) to find the energy supplied. Record the final temperature. Calculate c = ΔE ÷ (mΔθ). Wrap the block in insulation to reduce energy lost to the surroundings, which otherwise makes the value of c too high.

**Supplement — measuring c of a liquid:** the same method, with the heater and thermometer in a known mass of liquid in an insulated beaker, stirring so the temperature is even.

### Melting, boiling and evaporation — 2.2.3

During melting and boiling, energy is supplied but the temperature stays constant until the change of state is complete. The energy is used to break the forces between the particles (it increases the internal energy without raising the average kinetic energy). On a heating graph these show as flat sections.

At standard atmospheric pressure, pure water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C.

Condensation: gas particles slow down as they cool, come close together and are held by attractive forces to form a liquid; energy is released. Solidification: liquid particles lose energy and settle into fixed positions in a regular arrangement.

Evaporation is the escape of the more energetic particles from the surface of a liquid. The particles left behind have a lower average kinetic energy, so the liquid cools.

**Supplement — boiling compared with evaporation:**

| Boiling | Evaporation |
|---|---|
| Happens at one temperature (the boiling point) | Happens at any temperature |
| Happens throughout the liquid (bubbles form) | Happens only at the surface |
| Needs a continuous energy supply to continue | Can happen without heating; the liquid cools |

**Supplement — what speeds up evaporation:** a higher temperature (more particles have enough energy to escape), a larger surface area (more particles are at the surface), and air movement over the surface (wind carries escaped particles away so fewer return). Wet clothes dry fastest on a warm, windy day, spread out.

**Supplement — cooling by evaporation:** sweat on skin evaporates; the most energetic particles escape, the remaining liquid cools and takes energy from the skin, so the skin cools. Alcohol on the hand feels cold for the same reason.

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| c = ΔE ÷ (mΔθ) | c: specific heat capacity, J/(kg °C); ΔE: energy supplied, J; m: mass, kg; Δθ: temperature change, °C | Heating or cooling with no change of state. Rearranged: ΔE = mcΔθ, Δθ = ΔE ÷ (mc), m = ΔE ÷ (cΔθ). | Supplement |
| ΔE = Pt | P: heater power, W; t: time, s | Energy supplied by an electrical heater (from Unit 6). | Core |
| ΔE = IVt | I: current, A; V: p.d., V; t: s | Energy supplied, measured with ammeter, voltmeter and timer (from Unit 15). | Core |
| Δθ = final temperature − initial temperature | °C (same size as K) | Always a temperature change, never a single temperature. | Supplement |

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: energy to heat something (Supplement)

**Example — kettle.** How much energy heats 1.5 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C? (c = 4200 J/(kg °C))

- Δθ = 100 − 20 = 80 °C.
- ΔE = mcΔθ = 1.5 × 4200 × 80 = 504 000 J = 504 kJ.

### Situation: find the temperature rise (Supplement)

**Example — copper block.** 7800 J is supplied to a 0.50 kg copper block (c = 390 J/(kg °C)).

- Δθ = ΔE ÷ (mc) = 7800 ÷ (0.50 × 390) = 40 °C.
- If it started at 22 °C, it ends at 62 °C.

### Situation: find c from an experiment (Supplement)

**Example — heater and block.** A 50 W heater runs for 300 s in a 1.0 kg aluminium block. The temperature rises from 20 °C to 35 °C.

- ΔE = Pt = 50 × 300 = 15 000 J.
- c = 15 000 ÷ (1.0 × 15) = 1000 J/(kg °C).
- The accepted value is about 900 J/(kg °C). The experimental value is high because some energy escaped to the surroundings, so the temperature rose less than it should.

**Example — with meters.** A heater has 12 V across it and 4.0 A through it for 5 minutes, heating 2.0 kg of a liquid by 6.0 °C.

- ΔE = IVt = 4.0 × 12 × 300 = 14 400 J.
- c = 14 400 ÷ (2.0 × 6.0) = 1200 J/(kg °C).

### Situation: find the time (Supplement)

**Example — immersion heater.** How long does a 3000 W heater take to warm 100 kg of water by 40 °C, assuming no energy is lost?

- ΔE = 100 × 4200 × 40 = 16 800 000 J.
- t = ΔE ÷ P = 16 800 000 ÷ 3000 = 5600 s, about 93 minutes.

### Situation: cooling (Supplement)

**Example — energy released.** 0.20 kg of water cools from 80 °C to 30 °C. Energy released = 0.20 × 4200 × 50 = 42 000 J.

### Situation: reading a heating curve

**Example — interpreting the graph.** Ice at −10 °C is heated steadily. The graph rises to 0 °C, stays flat while the ice melts, rises to 100 °C, then stays flat while the water boils. On the flat parts energy is still going in, but it breaks the forces between particles instead of raising the temperature. The boiling section is longer than the melting section because separating particles completely takes much more energy than loosening them.

## Exam checks

- Δθ is a change in temperature: subtract. A change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K.
- Mass in kg in c = ΔE ÷ (mΔθ): 500 g = 0.50 kg.
- A temperature that stops rising while energy is supplied means a change of state.
- Evaporation cools because the most energetic particles leave, lowering the average kinetic energy of those left.
- Measured values of c are usually too high because of energy lost to the surroundings; suggest insulation and a lid.
- Expansion order: gases most, then liquids, then solids, explained by the forces between particles.
