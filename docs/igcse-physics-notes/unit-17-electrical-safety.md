# Unit 17 — Electrical safety

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, section 4.4, Core. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Hazards — 4.4

- **Damaged insulation:** exposed live wires can give an electric shock if touched, or cause a short circuit and fire.
- **Overheating cables:** a cable carrying more current than it is designed for gets hot; the insulation can melt and start a fire. Thin cables overheat at lower currents than thick ones.
- **Damp conditions:** water (especially impure water) conducts, so it can provide a path for current through a person; wet skin has a lower resistance, so a shock is more dangerous.
- **Overloading:** plugging too many appliances into one socket, extension lead or adaptor draws a large total current, which overheats the cables and plug, risking fire.

### Mains wiring — 4.4

A mains circuit has three wires:

- **Live (line) wire:** carries the alternating p.d. (about 230 V in many countries). It is the dangerous wire.
- **Neutral wire:** completes the circuit; it is kept at or near 0 V.
- **Earth wire:** a safety wire connected to the ground; it normally carries no current.

Switches (and fuses) must be connected in the live wire. Then, when the switch is off, the appliance is disconnected from the high p.d. If the switch were in the neutral wire, the appliance would still be connected to the live wire even when switched off, and touching a fault inside it could give a shock.

### Fuses and trip switches — 4.4

A **fuse** is a thin wire in a cartridge. If the current rises above its rating, the wire heats up and melts, breaking the circuit. It protects the cables and appliance from overheating and fire. Once blown, it must be replaced.

A **trip switch (circuit breaker)** uses an electromagnet: a current above its setting makes the electromagnet strong enough to pull a switch open. It acts faster than a fuse and can be reset by pressing a button once the fault is fixed.

**Choosing a rating:** pick the smallest standard value that is just above the normal working current. Common fuse ratings are 3 A, 5 A and 13 A. A fuse rated too low blows during normal use; one rated far too high does not protect against a fault.

### Earthing and double insulation — 4.4

An appliance with a metal casing must be **earthed**: the earth wire connects the casing to the ground. If a fault lets the live wire touch the casing, a large current flows through the low-resistance earth wire. This blows the fuse (or trips the breaker), disconnecting the live supply, so the casing is never left at a dangerous p.d. that could give a shock.

A **double-insulated** appliance has a casing made of plastic (an insulator), and its live parts are insulated a second time inside. The casing cannot become live, so no earth wire is needed. A double-insulated appliance still has a fuse in the live wire: the fuse (without an earth wire) protects the circuit and the cable from overheating if too much current flows. The symbol is a square inside a square.

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| I = P ÷ V | I: normal current, A; P: power rating, W; V: mains p.d., V | Find the working current of an appliance, then choose a fuse. From P = IV (Unit 15). | Core |
| fuse rating = next standard value above the working current | A | Choosing a fuse or trip-switch setting. | Core |
| total current = sum of the currents of all appliances on the socket | A | Deciding whether a socket or extension lead is overloaded. | Core |

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: choose a fuse

**Example — kettle.** A 2.8 kW kettle on 230 V. I = 2800 ÷ 230 = 12.2 A. Choose a 13 A fuse (3 A and 5 A would blow at once).

**Example — lamp.** A 60 W lamp on 230 V. I = 60 ÷ 230 = 0.26 A. Choose a 3 A fuse.

**Example — television.** A 920 W appliance on 230 V. I = 920 ÷ 230 = 4.0 A. Choose a 5 A fuse.

### Situation: overloaded extension lead

**Example.** An extension lead rated 13 A has a 2.0 kW heater, a 1.2 kW iron and a 0.3 kW computer plugged in, on 230 V.

- Total power = 3.5 kW = 3500 W.
- Total current = 3500 ÷ 230 = 15.2 A, above 13 A, so the lead is overloaded and its fuse should blow (or it may overheat).

### Situation: trip switch setting

**Example.** A lighting circuit has ten 100 W lamps on 230 V. Total current = 1000 ÷ 230 = 4.3 A. A 6 A trip switch would be suitable; a 32 A one would not protect the thin lighting cables.

### Situation: explaining a fault

**Example — live wire touches a metal casing.** With an earth wire: a large current flows from live to earth, the fuse melts, the supply is cut off, and the casing is safe. Without an earth wire: the casing stays at the live p.d.; a person touching it provides a path to earth and gets a shock.

## Exam checks

- Switches and fuses go in the live wire. Explain why: off means disconnected from the high p.d.
- Earthing works together with the fuse: the earth gives a low-resistance path, the large current blows the fuse.
- A fuse protects the cables and appliance from overheating; it does not prevent a shock on its own.
- Choose the fuse just above the working current, not equal to it and not far above it.
- Double-insulated: plastic casing, no earth wire needed, but a fuse is still used.
