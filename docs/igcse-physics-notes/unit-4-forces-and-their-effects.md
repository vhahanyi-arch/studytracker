# Unit 4 — Forces and their effects

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 1.5.1, 1.5.2 and 1.5.3, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Forces change shape and motion — 1.5.1

A force can change the size and shape of an object (stretch, squash, bend, twist) and can change its motion. A resultant force changes an object's velocity: it can speed it up, slow it down or change its direction.

If the resultant force is zero, an object at rest stays at rest, and a moving object carries on in a straight line at constant speed. (This is Newton's first law.) A car cruising at a steady 30 m/s on a straight road has a driving force exactly balanced by friction and air resistance.

### Resultant of forces along a line — 1.5.1

Forces acting along the same line add if they point the same way and subtract if they point in opposite directions. Choose one direction as positive. A 50 N push forward and a 20 N friction backward give a resultant of 30 N forward.

### Friction and drag — 1.5.1

Solid friction is the force between two surfaces that may impede (oppose) motion and produces heating; rub your hands together and they get warm. Drag is friction on an object moving through a liquid (water resistance) or a gas (air resistance). Drag increases as the speed increases.

### Stretching springs — 1.5.1

For a load–extension experiment, clamp a spring above a ruler, record the position of the bottom of the spring, add masses one at a time and record the new position each time. Extension = new length − original length, never the total length. Weight of each load = mg.

The graph of load against extension is a straight line through the origin at first: the extension is proportional to the load (Hooke's law). **Supplement:** the point where the line stops being straight is the limit of proportionality. Beyond it, extension is no longer proportional to load.

**Supplement:** the spring constant k is the force per unit extension. A stiff spring has a large k. On a load (y-axis) against extension (x-axis) graph, k is the gradient of the straight part.

### Force, mass and acceleration — 1.5.1 (Supplement)

A resultant force F on a mass m produces an acceleration a in the same direction as the force: F = ma. Double the resultant force and the acceleration doubles; double the mass and the acceleration halves. Always use the resultant force, not just the driving force.

### Motion in a circle — 1.5.1 (Supplement)

An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating, because its direction keeps changing. It needs a force perpendicular to its motion, towards the centre of the circle (tension in a string, friction on car tyres, gravity on a satellite). If the force stops, the object moves off in a straight line along the tangent.

- With mass and radius fixed, a larger force means a higher speed.
- With mass and speed fixed, a larger force means a smaller radius.
- With speed and radius fixed, a larger mass needs a larger force.

(The formula F = mv²/r is not required.)

### Turning effect: moments — 1.5.2

The moment of a force is its turning effect about a pivot: moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force. Unit: N m. Everyday examples: a spanner (a longer handle gives a bigger moment for the same force), a door handle placed far from the hinges, a see-saw, a wheelbarrow.

Principle of moments: for an object in equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments about that point.

An object is in equilibrium when there is no resultant force and no resultant moment. Then it neither starts moving nor starts turning.

**Supplement — experiment:** balance a metre rule on a pivot at its centre. Hang different weights at measured distances on each side until the rule balances again. For each weight calculate force × distance; the total clockwise moment equals the total anticlockwise moment, showing there is no resultant moment.

### Centre of gravity and stability — 1.5.3

The centre of gravity is the point through which the whole weight of an object can be taken to act.

To find it for an irregular flat sheet (lamina): make three small holes near the edge. Hang the lamina from a pin through one hole so it swings freely, hang a plumb line from the same pin, and draw along the plumb line. Repeat for the other holes. The centre of gravity is where the lines cross (the third line checks the first two).

An object is stable when its centre of gravity is low and its base is wide. If it is tilted, its weight acts through the base and produces a moment that turns it back. It topples when it is tilted so far that the line of action of its weight falls outside the base. Racing cars are low and wide; a double-decker bus keeps heavy parts low.

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| resultant = sum of forces in one direction − sum in the opposite direction | N | Forces along one straight line. | Core |
| extension = stretched length − original length | m or cm | Every spring or rubber-band question. | Core |
| moment = F × d | F: force, N; d: perpendicular distance from pivot, m; moment: N m | Turning effect of one force. | Core |
| sum of clockwise moments = sum of anticlockwise moments | N m | Balanced beams and see-saws in equilibrium (Core: one force each side; Supplement: several). | Core |
| k = F ÷ x | k: spring constant, N/m (or N/cm); F: load, N; x: extension, m | Up to the limit of proportionality only. Rearranged: F = kx, x = F ÷ k. | Supplement |
| F = ma | F: resultant force, N; m: mass, kg; a: acceleration, m/s² | Resultant force and acceleration, same direction. Rearranged: a = F ÷ m, m = F ÷ a. | Supplement |

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: forces along a line

**Example — tug of war.** Team A pulls left with 820 N; team B pulls right with 760 N. Resultant = 820 − 760 = 60 N to the left, so the rope accelerates to the left.

**Example — steady speed.** A lorry moves at constant velocity with a driving force of 4500 N. The total resistive force must also be 4500 N, because constant velocity means zero resultant force.

### Situation: springs (Supplement for k)

**Example — spring constant.** A spring of length 12.0 cm stretches to 15.0 cm under a 6.0 N load.

- Extension x = 15.0 − 12.0 = 3.0 cm = 0.030 m.
- k = F ÷ x = 6.0 ÷ 0.030 = 200 N/m.

**Example — predict an extension.** The same spring has a 4.0 N load. x = F ÷ k = 4.0 ÷ 200 = 0.020 m = 2.0 cm (as long as the limit of proportionality is not passed).

**Example — from the graph.** The straight part of a load–extension graph passes through (0.050 m, 2.5 N). Gradient = k = 2.5 ÷ 0.050 = 50 N/m.

### Situation: F = ma with one force (Supplement)

**Example — acceleration.** A resultant force of 1200 N acts on a 800 kg car. a = 1200 ÷ 800 = 1.5 m/s².

**Example — force needed.** A 0.15 kg ball is to be given an acceleration of 40 m/s². F = 0.15 × 40 = 6.0 N.

### Situation: F = ma with friction (Supplement)

**Example — resultant first.** A 50 kg crate is pushed with 180 N against 80 N of friction.

- Resultant = 180 − 80 = 100 N.
- a = 100 ÷ 50 = 2.0 m/s².

**Example — braking force.** A 1000 kg car decelerates from 20 m/s to rest in 4.0 s.

- a = (0 − 20) ÷ 4.0 = −5.0 m/s².
- F = ma = 1000 × (−5.0) = −5000 N: a 5000 N braking force opposite to the motion.

**Example — lift or rocket.** A 2000 kg rocket has a thrust of 30 000 N (g = 9.8 N/kg).

- Weight = 2000 × 9.8 = 19 600 N.
- Resultant upward force = 30 000 − 19 600 = 10 400 N.
- a = 10 400 ÷ 2000 = 5.2 m/s² upward.

### Situation: one force each side of a pivot

**Example — see-saw.** A 400 N child sits 1.5 m from the pivot. Where must a 300 N child sit to balance?

- Anticlockwise moment = 400 × 1.5 = 600 N m.
- 300 × d = 600, so d = 2.0 m on the other side.

**Example — spanner.** A 25 N force at the end of a 0.20 m spanner gives a moment of 25 × 0.20 = 5.0 N m. Holding it at 0.10 m would need 50 N for the same turning effect.

### Situation: several forces each side (Supplement)

**Example — metre rule.** A uniform metre rule is pivoted at its centre (50 cm mark). A 2.0 N weight hangs at the 10 cm mark and a 3.0 N weight at the 30 cm mark. Where must a 5.0 N weight hang on the other side?

- Anticlockwise moments: 2.0 × 0.40 + 3.0 × 0.20 = 0.80 + 0.60 = 1.40 N m.
- Clockwise: 5.0 × d = 1.40, so d = 0.28 m from the pivot, at the 78 cm mark.

**Example — beam with its own weight.** A uniform 4.0 m plank weighing 200 N rests on a pivot 1.0 m from its left end. What downward force at the left end balances it?

- The plank's weight acts at its centre, 2.0 m from the left end, which is 1.0 m to the right of the pivot: clockwise moment = 200 × 1.0 = 200 N m.
- Force at the left end, 1.0 m from the pivot: F × 1.0 = 200, so F = 200 N.

**Example — force on the pivot.** In equilibrium the upward force from the pivot equals the total downward force (no resultant force). For the plank above, the pivot pushes up with 200 + 200 = 400 N.

## Exam checks

- Use the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of the force, in metres for N m.
- In a moments question, take moments about the pivot so the pivot's own force has no moment.
- Equilibrium needs both: no resultant force and no resultant moment.
- Extension is the increase in length, not the new length.
- F in F = ma is the resultant force. Subtract friction or weight first.
- Explain toppling with the line of action of the weight falling outside the base.
