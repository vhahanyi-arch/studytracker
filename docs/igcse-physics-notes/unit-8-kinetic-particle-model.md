# Unit 8 — Kinetic particle model of matter

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 2.1.1, 2.1.2 and 2.1.3, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### States of matter — 2.1.1

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Fixed | Takes the shape of the container | Fills the whole container |
| Volume | Fixed | Fixed | Fills the container; easily changed |
| Compressible? | No (hardly) | No (hardly) | Yes, easily |
| Flows? | No | Yes | Yes |

Changes of state: melting (solid → liquid), boiling or evaporation (liquid → gas), condensation (gas → liquid), solidification or freezing (liquid → solid).

### The particle model — 2.1.2

- **Solid:** particles closely packed in a regular arrangement (a lattice). They vibrate about fixed positions. Strong forces between them.
- **Liquid:** particles close together but irregularly arranged. They move around each other (slide past one another). Forces are weaker than in a solid.
- **Gas:** particles far apart (about ten times further than in a liquid), randomly arranged, moving quickly in random directions in straight lines between collisions. Forces between them are very small.

In a particle diagram, draw solids as touching circles in neat rows, liquids as mostly touching circles with gaps and no pattern, and gases as a few widely spaced circles.

**Supplement:** the forces and distances between particles (atoms, molecules, ions, electrons) and the way the particles move explain the properties. Strong forces and fixed positions give a solid its fixed shape; particles that can move past each other let a liquid flow; large spaces between gas particles make a gas easy to compress.

### Temperature and particle motion — 2.1.2

The higher the temperature, the faster the particles move on average: temperature is linked to the average kinetic energy of the particles. As a substance is cooled, its particles slow down. At absolute zero, −273 °C, the particles have the least possible kinetic energy. Nothing can be colder.

### Gas pressure — 2.1.2

Gas particles constantly collide with the walls of their container. Each collision exerts a small force on the wall; the many collisions together produce the pressure.

**Supplement:** each particle changes momentum when it rebounds from the wall, so it exerts a force on the wall. The total force from all the collisions divided by the wall's area is the pressure (force per unit area).

### Brownian motion — 2.1.2

Smoke particles in air (or pollen grains in water) seen through a microscope move in a jerky, random, zigzag way. This is evidence for the kinetic particle model: the visible particles are being hit unevenly, from all sides, by the much smaller, fast-moving air or water molecules that cannot be seen.

**Supplement:** the air molecules are light and fast. A large, heavy smoke particle is moved only slightly by each collision, but at any instant more molecules hit one side than the other, so it is pushed in a changing, random direction. Use "molecules" or "atoms" for the air or water, and "microscopic particles" for the smoke or pollen.

### Gases: pressure, volume and temperature — 2.1.3

For a fixed mass of gas:

- **Temperature up, volume fixed → pressure up.** The particles move faster, hit the walls more often and harder, so the force per unit area increases.
- **Volume down, temperature fixed → pressure up.** The particles are closer together and hit the walls more often (the average speed is unchanged), so the pressure increases.

The kelvin (absolute) scale starts at absolute zero: 0 K = −273 °C. A change of 1 K is the same size as a change of 1 °C.

**Supplement:** at constant temperature, pressure × volume is constant (Boyle's law). Halve the volume and the pressure doubles. A graph of p against V is a curve (inverse proportion); a graph of p against 1/V is a straight line through the origin.

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| T = θ + 273 | T: temperature, K; θ: temperature, °C | Converting °C to kelvin. Backwards: θ = T − 273. | Core |
| pV = constant | p: pressure, Pa (or any pressure unit); V: volume, m³ (or any volume unit) | Fixed mass of gas at constant temperature. | Supplement |
| p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ | 1: before the change; 2: after | The working form of pV = constant. The units only need to match on both sides. | Supplement |

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: converting temperatures

**Example — Celsius to kelvin.** Water boils at 100 °C = 100 + 273 = 373 K. Body temperature 37 °C = 310 K.

**Example — kelvin to Celsius.** Liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K = 77 − 273 = −196 °C.

**Example — a temperature change.** Heating from 20 °C to 50 °C is a rise of 30 °C, which is also a rise of 30 K. Do not add 273 to a change.

### Situation: compressing a gas (Supplement)

**Example — find the new pressure.** A syringe holds 60 cm³ of air at 100 kPa. The plunger is pushed in slowly (constant temperature) until the volume is 20 cm³.

- p₁V₁ = p₂V₂: 100 × 60 = p₂ × 20.
- p₂ = 6000 ÷ 20 = 300 kPa. A third of the volume gives three times the pressure.

### Situation: a gas that expands (Supplement)

**Example — rising bubble.** A bubble of volume 2.0 cm³ is released at the bottom of a lake where the pressure is 250 kPa. At the surface the pressure is 100 kPa. Assume constant temperature.

- 250 × 2.0 = 100 × V₂, so V₂ = 5.0 cm³. The bubble grows as the pressure falls.

### Situation: find the original volume (Supplement)

**Example — gas cylinder.** Gas from a cylinder of volume 0.050 m³ at 2 000 000 Pa is released into the air at 100 000 Pa (same temperature). What volume does it take up?

- V₂ = p₁V₁ ÷ p₂ = 2 000 000 × 0.050 ÷ 100 000 = 1.0 m³.

### Situation: reading a pV graph (Supplement)

**Example — check the law.** Readings: (p = 100 kPa, V = 30 cm³), (150 kPa, 20 cm³), (300 kPa, 10 cm³). Each product pV = 3000 kPa cm³, so pressure is inversely proportional to volume.

## Exam checks

- Describe particles with three ideas: arrangement, separation, motion.
- Explain gas pressure with collisions with the walls: more frequent and harder collisions mean a larger force per unit area.
- Compressing at constant temperature: the collisions become more frequent, not harder.
- Brownian motion: the moving thing you see is the smoke particle, hit by unseen air molecules.
- Use pV = constant only when the mass of gas and the temperature are both fixed.
- Absolute zero is −273 °C, which is 0 K.
