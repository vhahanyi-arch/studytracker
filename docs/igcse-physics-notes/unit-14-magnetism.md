# Unit 14 — Magnetism

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, section 4.1, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Magnetic poles and forces — 4.1

Every magnet has a north (N) pole and a south (S) pole, where the magnetic effect is strongest.

- Like poles repel: N–N or S–S push apart.
- Unlike poles attract: N–S pull together.
- A magnet attracts unmagnetised magnetic materials (iron, steel, nickel, cobalt), whichever pole is used.

Repulsion is the only sure test that an object is a magnet: an unmagnetised piece of iron is attracted by both poles, but a magnet is repelled by one of them.

A material is **magnetised** when it behaves as a magnet, and **unmagnetised** when it does not. **Magnetic materials** are attracted by magnets and can be magnetised (iron, steel, nickel, cobalt). **Non-magnetic materials** are not attracted (copper, aluminium, wood, plastic, glass).

### Induced magnetism — 4.1

A magnetic material placed near or touching a magnet becomes a magnet itself: its magnetism is induced. The end nearest the magnet's N pole becomes an S pole, which is why it is attracted. A chain of paper clips can hang from a magnet because each clip induces magnetism in the next.

### Temporary and permanent magnets — 4.1

| Soft iron | Steel |
|---|---|
| Easy to magnetise | Harder to magnetise |
| Loses its magnetism easily when removed from the field (temporary magnet) | Keeps its magnetism (permanent magnet) |
| Used in electromagnets, transformer cores, relays | Used in compasses, fridge magnets, loudspeakers, motors |

### Magnetic fields — 4.1

A magnetic field is a region in which a magnetic pole experiences a force. The direction of the field at a point is the direction of the force on a north pole placed there. So field lines point away from N poles and towards S poles.

Field pattern of a bar magnet: lines come out of the N pole, curve round, and go into the S pole. They never cross. They are closest together near the poles, where the field is strongest.

Between two facing N poles the lines push away from each other, leaving a neutral point midway where there is no field. Between a facing N and S pole the lines run straight across, giving a nearly uniform field.

**Plotting the field:**

- **Plotting compass:** put the magnet on paper. Place a small compass near the N pole and mark the position the needle's N end points to. Move the compass so its tail is on the mark, mark again, and continue to the S pole. Join the dots to form one line, with an arrow; repeat from other starting points. The compass also shows the field's direction.
- **Iron filings:** put paper over the magnet, sprinkle filings and tap gently. The filings line up along the field lines, showing the pattern quickly, but not the direction.

### Uses of magnets — 4.1

- **Permanent magnets:** compasses, fridge door seals, loudspeakers, microphones, electric motors and generators, magnetic catches.
- **Electromagnets** (a coil of wire around a soft-iron core, magnetic only when current flows): scrap-yard cranes that can drop their load by switching off, relays, electric bells, magnetic door locks, MRI scanners. Their strength can be varied by changing the current.

**Supplement:** magnetic forces are due to interactions between magnetic fields: the field of one magnet acts on the other. The relative strength of a field is shown by the spacing of the field lines: closer lines mean a stronger field.

## Using the ideas in different situations

### Situation: is it a magnet?

**Example — three bars.** Bar A repels one end of a known magnet; bar B is attracted by both ends; bar C is not affected. A is a magnet; B is an unmagnetised magnetic material (such as iron); C is non-magnetic (such as aluminium).

### Situation: drawing field lines

**Example — two magnets.** Two bar magnets lie end to end with N facing S. Draw lines running straight from the N pole across to the S pole in the gap, close together and evenly spaced (uniform, strong). With N facing N, the lines curve away from each other, and there is a neutral point in the middle with no lines.

### Situation: choosing a material

**Example — crane.** A scrap-yard crane uses an electromagnet with a soft-iron core: soft iron magnetises strongly when current flows and loses its magnetism when the current is switched off, releasing the scrap. Steel would stay magnetised and keep holding on.

### Situation: induced magnetism

**Example — which pole?** The N pole of a magnet is brought near the left end of an iron nail. The left end becomes an S pole and the right end an N pole, so the nail is attracted. A second nail touching the right end becomes magnetised in the same way.

## Exam checks

- Only repulsion proves an object is a magnet.
- Arrows on field lines go from N to S outside the magnet.
- Field lines never cross; they are densest at the poles.
- Iron filings show the pattern; a compass shows the pattern and the direction.
- Soft iron for temporary magnets (electromagnets, transformers); steel for permanent magnets.
