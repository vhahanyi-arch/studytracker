# Unit 11 — General properties of waves

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, section 3.1, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### What a wave is — 3.1

Waves transfer energy from one place to another without transferring matter. When a wave passes along a rope, each part of the rope vibrates about a fixed position; the rope itself does not travel along. A cork on a pond bobs up and down as ripples pass; it is not carried to the shore.

Wave motion can be shown with a rope or a slinky spring (shake one end), and with water waves in a ripple tank.

### Describing a wave — 3.1

- **Crest (peak):** the highest point of a transverse wave. **Trough:** the lowest point.
- **Amplitude:** the maximum displacement from the rest (undisturbed) position: from the middle to a crest, not crest to trough.
- **Wavelength, λ:** the distance between two neighbouring identical points on the wave, such as crest to crest. Unit: m.
- **Frequency, f:** the number of complete waves passing a point per second. Unit: hertz (Hz); 1 Hz = 1 wave per second.
- **Wave speed, v:** the distance a wave travels per second. Unit: m/s.
- **Wavefront:** a line joining points on the same part of a wave, such as all of one crest. In a ripple tank, wavefronts are the bright lines; the distance between neighbouring wavefronts is one wavelength.
- **Period, T:** the time for one complete wave to pass a point. T = 1 ÷ f.

### Transverse and longitudinal waves — 3.1

**Transverse:** the vibrations are at right angles to the direction the wave travels (propagates). Examples: all electromagnetic waves (light, radio, X-rays), water waves, seismic S-waves (secondary).

**Longitudinal:** the vibrations are parallel to the direction the wave travels, producing compressions (particles close together) and rarefactions (particles spread out). Examples: sound waves, seismic P-waves (primary). A slinky pushed in and out along its length shows a longitudinal wave; shaken side to side, it shows a transverse wave.

### Reflection, refraction and diffraction — 3.1

- **Reflection at a plane surface:** the wave bounces off. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection; wavelength, frequency and speed are unchanged.
- **Refraction:** a change of direction when a wave changes speed as it enters a different medium (or, for water waves, a different depth), arriving at an angle. Water waves slow down in shallow water, so their wavelength gets shorter; the frequency stays the same, because it is set by the source.
- **Diffraction:** waves spread out as they pass through a gap or past an edge. Wavelength, frequency and speed are unchanged.

**Ripple tank demonstrations:** a straight vibrating bar makes plane (straight) waves. Place a flat barrier at an angle to show reflection. Place a glass plate on the bottom to make a shallow region: the wavefronts get closer together (shorter wavelength, slower) and bend at an angled boundary. Place two barriers with a gap to show diffraction through a gap, or one barrier to show diffraction at an edge. A lamp above the tank projects the wavefronts onto paper below.

**Supplement — how much diffraction:**

- Through a gap: diffraction is greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength. A gap much wider than the wavelength gives only slight spreading at the edges. For a fixed gap, a longer wavelength diffracts more.
- At an edge: longer wavelengths bend further round the edge than shorter ones.

This is why you can hear (long wavelength sound) round a corner but not see (very short wavelength light) round it, and why long-wave radio signals reach valleys that short-wave signals do not.

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| v = fλ | v: wave speed, m/s; f: frequency, Hz; λ (lambda): wavelength, m | Every type of wave. Rearranged: f = v ÷ λ, λ = v ÷ f. | Core |
| v = distance ÷ time | m/s | Speed from how far a wavefront moves in a measured time. | Core |
| f = number of waves ÷ time | Hz | Counting waves passing a point. | Core |
| T = 1 ÷ f | T: period, s; f: Hz | Time for one wave, for example from an oscilloscope trace. Useful, not a listed syllabus equation. | Core |

Prefixes: kHz = 10³ Hz, MHz = 10⁶ Hz, GHz = 10⁹ Hz; mm = 10⁻³ m, nm = 10⁻⁹ m.

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: find the speed

**Example — water wave.** Waves of frequency 4.0 Hz and wavelength 0.15 m. v = fλ = 4.0 × 0.15 = 0.60 m/s.

### Situation: find the wavelength

**Example — radio.** A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz; radio waves travel at 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

- λ = v ÷ f = 3.0 × 10⁸ ÷ (100 × 10⁶) = 3.0 m.

**Example — sound.** A 440 Hz note in air (speed 330 m/s): λ = 330 ÷ 440 = 0.75 m.

### Situation: find the frequency

**Example — light.** Red light has a wavelength of 7.0 × 10⁻⁷ m. f = v ÷ λ = 3.0 × 10⁸ ÷ (7.0 × 10⁻⁷) = 4.3 × 10¹⁴ Hz.

### Situation: information from a diagram

**Example — counting waves.** A diagram shows 3 complete waves over a distance of 1.2 m, and 6 waves pass a point every 2.0 s.

- λ = 1.2 ÷ 3 = 0.40 m.
- f = 6 ÷ 2.0 = 3.0 Hz.
- v = 3.0 × 0.40 = 1.2 m/s.

**Example — reading the amplitude.** A transverse wave drawing has crests 8 cm above troughs. The amplitude is half the crest-to-trough height: 4 cm.

### Situation: refraction into shallow water

**Example — slower, same frequency.** Waves of frequency 5.0 Hz travel at 0.30 m/s in deep water and 0.20 m/s in shallow water.

- In deep water: λ = 0.30 ÷ 5.0 = 0.060 m = 6.0 cm.
- In shallow water: λ = 0.20 ÷ 5.0 = 0.040 m = 4.0 cm. The frequency stays 5.0 Hz.

### Situation: wavefronts in a ripple tank

**Example — from the spacing.** Ten wavefronts cover 12 cm on the screen and the vibrator runs at 8.0 Hz. The ten wavefronts span nine gaps, so λ = 12 ÷ 9 = 1.33 cm = 0.0133 m; v = 8.0 × 0.0133 = 0.11 m/s.

## Exam checks

- Waves transfer energy, not matter.
- Amplitude is measured from the middle (rest position), not crest to trough.
- Convert prefixes before using v = fλ (MHz to Hz, cm to m).
- In refraction the frequency is unchanged; speed and wavelength change together.
- Diffraction is most noticeable when the gap is similar in size to the wavelength.
- Transverse: vibration at 90° to travel. Longitudinal: vibration along the direction of travel, with compressions and rarefactions.
