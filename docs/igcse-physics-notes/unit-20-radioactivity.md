# Unit 20 — Radioactivity

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 5.2.1, 5.2.2, 5.2.3, 5.2.4 and 5.2.5, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Background radiation and detection — 5.2.1

Background radiation is the low level of ionising radiation that is around us all the time, from natural and artificial sources. Significant sources:

- radon gas in the air (from rocks in the ground; often the largest contributor);
- rocks and buildings (granite, bricks);
- food and drink (all living things contain some radioactive isotopes, such as carbon-14 and potassium-40);
- cosmic rays from space.

Ionising radiation is measured with a detector (such as a Geiger–Müller tube) connected to a counter or ratemeter. The count rate is the number of counts per second (counts/s) or per minute (counts/minute).

**Supplement:** a measured count rate includes the background. Measure the background count rate with the source removed (ideally over a long time), then subtract it: corrected count rate = measured count rate − background count rate.

### The three types of nuclear emission — 5.2.2

Emission from a nucleus is spontaneous (it happens by itself; nothing triggers it) and random in direction.

| | Alpha (α) | Beta (β) | Gamma (γ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons | Fast-moving electron, emitted from the nucleus | Electromagnetic radiation (a very short wavelength wave) |
| Charge | +2 | −1 | 0 |
| Relative mass | 4 | about 1/1840 | 0 |
| Ionising effect | Strong | Moderate | Weak |
| Penetration (stopped by) | Weak: a sheet of paper, a few cm of air, skin | Moderate: a few mm of aluminium | Strong: reduced (never fully stopped) by thick lead or several metres of concrete |

(β here always means β⁻; β⁺ is not included.)

**Supplement — deflection in electric and magnetic fields:**

- Alpha and beta particles are charged, so they are deflected; they bend in opposite directions because their charges are opposite.
- Beta particles are deflected much more than alpha particles, because their mass is so much smaller (even though their charge is half the size).
- Gamma rays are uncharged, so they are not deflected.
- In an electric field between charged plates: alpha bends towards the negative plate, beta towards the positive plate. In a magnetic field: use Fleming's left-hand rule, treating beta as a current in the opposite direction to its motion.

**Supplement — why ionising effects differ:** ionisation happens when the radiation knocks electrons off atoms. Alpha particles have a large charge (+2) and, being heavy, travel relatively slowly with a lot of kinetic energy, so they interact strongly with every atom they pass and cause a great deal of ionisation. They lose their kinetic energy quickly, which is why they penetrate so little. Beta particles have less charge (−1) and move faster, so they ionise less and travel further. Gamma rays have no charge, so they ionise least and penetrate most.

### Radioactive decay — 5.2.3

Radioactive decay is a change in an unstable nucleus that results in the emission of alpha or beta particles and/or gamma radiation. It is spontaneous and random: you cannot predict which nucleus will decay next, or when, and nothing (temperature, pressure, chemical reaction) changes the rate.

During alpha or beta decay the number of protons changes, so the nucleus becomes a different element.

**Supplement — why nuclei are unstable:** an isotope may be radioactive because it has too many neutrons for its number of protons, or because the nucleus is too heavy (too many nucleons). Decay makes the nucleus more stable.

- **Alpha decay:** the nucleus loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons. Nucleon number falls by 4; proton number falls by 2. Typical of heavy nuclei.
- **Beta decay:** a neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton and an electron; the electron is emitted at high speed. Nucleon number stays the same; proton number rises by 1. This reduces the number of excess neutrons.
- **Gamma emission:** the nucleus loses energy as a gamma ray, often just after alpha or beta decay. The nucleon and proton numbers do not change.

**Supplement — decay equations:**

- Alpha: `^A_Z X → ^(A−4)_(Z−2) Y + ^4_2 α`
- Beta: `^A_Z X → ^A_(Z+1) Y + ^0_-1 β`
- Gamma: `^A_Z X → ^A_Z X + γ`
- In beta decay, the change inside the nucleus is `^1_0 n → ^1_1 p + ^0_-1 e`.

### Half-life — 5.2.4

The half-life of an isotope is the time taken for half the nuclei of that isotope in any sample to decay. After each half-life, the number of undecayed nuclei, the activity, and the count rate (after background is subtracted) all halve. Each isotope has its own half-life, from fractions of a second to billions of years.

After 1 half-life: ½ remains. After 2: ¼. After 3: ⅛. After 4: 1/16. After n half-lives: (½)ⁿ of the original remains.

On a decay curve (count rate against time), read the time for the count rate to fall from any value to half that value; repeat from different starting points and take the mean.

**Supplement:** if the data include background radiation, subtract the background from every reading first, then find the half-life. A curve that levels off at a constant non-zero value shows the background count rate.

**Supplement — choosing an isotope for a job:** the type of radiation decides what it can pass through and how much harm it does; the half-life decides how long it stays active.

| Application | Radiation needed | Half-life needed | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Household smoke alarm | Alpha | Long (years; americium-241 has about 430 years) | Alpha ionises the air in a small gap, letting a tiny current flow; smoke absorbs the alpha, the current drops and the alarm sounds. Alpha cannot escape the alarm's casing. A long half-life means no replacement is needed. |
| Irradiating food to kill bacteria | Gamma | Long enough to last (cobalt-60, about 5 years) | Gamma penetrates the packaging and the food and kills microorganisms; the food does not become radioactive. |
| Sterilising medical equipment | Gamma | Fairly long | Penetrates sealed packaging, so equipment stays sterile until opened. |
| Measuring and controlling thickness | Beta for paper, foil or thin plastic; gamma for thick metal sheets | Long, so the count rate does not fall during use | The source is on one side, a detector on the other. If the sheet is too thick, fewer particles get through, and the rollers adjust. Alpha would be stopped completely; gamma would pass through paper almost unaffected. |
| Diagnosing cancer (tracer) | Gamma | Short (hours; technetium-99m about 6 hours) | Gamma passes out of the body to a detector; a short half-life means the patient is not exposed for long. |
| Treating cancer | Gamma | Long, for the external source (cobalt-60) | Beams from several directions meet at the tumour to kill its cells, limiting the dose to healthy tissue. |

### Safety — 5.2.5

Ionising radiation can damage living cells: it can kill cells (cell death), change their DNA (mutations), and cause cancer. Alpha is most dangerous inside the body (swallowed or breathed in), because it is strongly ionising; outside the body, beta and gamma are more dangerous, because they can reach the organs.

Radioactive materials are moved, used and stored safely by:

- keeping them in lead-lined containers, labelled with the hazard symbol, locked away when not in use;
- handling them with long tongs, never with bare hands, and pointing them away from people;
- using them for the shortest possible time;
- wearing a film badge or dosimeter to monitor exposure (workers), and protective clothing or gloves.

**Supplement:** all these precautions work by:

- **reducing exposure time:** less time near the source means less radiation absorbed;
- **increasing distance:** radiation spreads out (and alpha and beta are absorbed by air), so the dose falls rapidly with distance;
- **using shielding:** materials such as lead, concrete or thick glass absorb the radiation before it reaches people.

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: how much is left

**Example — mass remaining.** A sample contains 80 g of an isotope with a half-life of 5 days. How much remains after 20 days?

- Number of half-lives = 20 ÷ 5 = 4.
- 80 → 40 → 20 → 10 → 5 g. (Or 80 × (½)⁴ = 5 g.)

**Example — count rate.** A source has a count rate of 640 counts/minute and a half-life of 2 hours. After 6 hours: 640 → 320 → 160 → 80 counts/minute.

### Situation: find the half-life from data

**Example — table.** A count rate falls from 1200 counts/s to 150 counts/s in 36 minutes.

- 1200 → 600 → 300 → 150 is 3 half-lives.
- Half-life = 36 ÷ 3 = 12 minutes.

**Example — fraction.** After 24 years, 1/8 of a sample remains. 1/8 = (½)³, so 3 half-lives = 24 years and the half-life is 8 years.

### Situation: find the time taken

**Example.** An isotope has a half-life of 3.0 hours. How long until its activity falls to 1/16 of the starting value? 1/16 = (½)⁴, so 4 × 3.0 = 12 hours.

### Situation: background radiation (Supplement)

**Example — corrected count rate.** A detector reads 85 counts/minute near a source; the background is 25 counts/minute. Corrected count rate = 85 − 25 = 60 counts/minute.

**Example — half-life with background.** The background is 20 counts/minute. A source reads 420 counts/minute at the start and 120 counts/minute after 10 minutes.

- Corrected: 420 − 20 = 400 at the start; 120 − 20 = 100 after 10 minutes.
- 400 → 200 → 100 is 2 half-lives, so the half-life = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 minutes.
- (Without correcting, 420 → 120 would not be a whole number of half-lives, and the answer would be wrong.)

### Situation: identify the radiation from absorption data

**Example.** Count rates (background subtracted): no absorber 500; paper 495; 3 mm aluminium 300; 3 mm lead 150. Paper barely changes the count, so there is no alpha. Aluminium removes some, so there is beta. Some still passes through aluminium and lead, so there is gamma too.

### Situation: decay equations (Supplement)

**Example — alpha decay.** Radium-226 (Z = 88) emits an alpha particle: `^226_88 Ra → ^222_86 Rn + ^4_2 α`. Nucleon number 226 = 222 + 4; proton number 88 = 86 + 2.

**Example — beta decay.** Carbon-14 (Z = 6) emits a beta particle: `^14_6 C → ^14_7 N + ^0_-1 β`. Nucleon number unchanged; proton number 6 = 7 + (−1).

**Example — a decay chain.** Uranium-238 (Z = 92) emits an alpha, then a beta, then another beta. After alpha: A = 234, Z = 90. After the first beta: A = 234, Z = 91. After the second beta: A = 234, Z = 92: an isotope of uranium again, `^234_92 U`.

## Exam checks

- Always subtract background before using count rates (Supplement), and say so.
- Half-life questions: count the halvings. Check the time units.
- Alpha: stopped by paper, most ionising. Beta: stopped by aluminium. Gamma: reduced by thick lead, least ionising.
- In fields, alpha and beta bend opposite ways, beta more; gamma goes straight.
- Alpha decay: A − 4, Z − 2. Beta decay: Z + 1, A the same. Gamma: no change.
- Choosing a source: match the penetration to the job, and the half-life to how long it is needed (short inside the body, long for equipment).
- Safety: time, distance, shielding.
