# Unit 21 — Space physics

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 6.1.1, 6.1.2, 6.2.1, 6.2.2 and 6.2.3, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### The Earth, Sun and Moon — 6.1.1

- **Day and night:** the Earth spins on its axis once in about 24 hours. The half facing the Sun has day; the other half has night. The Sun appears to rise in the east, cross the sky and set in the west because the Earth rotates.
- **Seasons:** the Earth orbits the Sun once in about 365 days, and its axis is tilted (by about 23.5°). When a hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, it has summer: the Sun is higher in the sky, its rays are more concentrated, and days are longer. Six months later, that hemisphere is tilted away and has winter.
- **Phases of the Moon:** the Moon orbits the Earth in about one month. We only see the part of its sunlit half that faces us, so as it moves round its orbit it appears to change shape: new moon, crescent, quarter, gibbous, full, and back again. The Moon gives out no light of its own; it reflects sunlight.

**Supplement:** the average orbital speed is the circumference of the orbit divided by the time for one orbit: v = 2πr ÷ T.

### The Solar System — 6.1.2

The Solar System contains:

- one star, the Sun;
- eight planets, in order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune (a memory aid: My Very Easy Method Just Speeds Up Naming);
- minor planets that orbit the Sun, including dwarf planets such as Pluto and the asteroids in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter;
- moons that orbit planets;
- smaller bodies, including comets (balls of ice and dust in very elongated orbits) and natural satellites.

The four inner planets are small and rocky; the four outer planets are large and gaseous. **Accretion model** of how the Solar System formed:

- It began as an interstellar cloud of gas and dust containing many elements.
- Gravity pulled the cloud together; as it collapsed, it began to rotate faster and flattened into a spinning accretion disc, with most of the mass at the centre forming the Sun.
- Close to the hot young Sun, only rocky, metallic materials with high melting points could stay solid, so small rocky planets formed there.
- Further out, where it was cold, ices and large amounts of gas (hydrogen and helium) were available and were not blown away, so large gaseous planets formed.

**Gravity in the Solar System:**

- The gravitational field strength at a planet's surface depends on the planet's mass (more mass, stronger field).
- The field around a planet gets weaker as the distance from the planet increases.
- The Sun contains most (over 99%) of the mass of the Solar System. Its gravitational attraction is the force that keeps the planets, and any object orbiting the Sun, in orbit.

Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach the Earth; light travels at 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

**Supplement:** planets, minor planets and comets move in elliptical (oval) orbits. The Sun is not at the centre of the ellipse but at one focus, off to one side; only when an orbit is nearly circular is the Sun close to its centre. Comets have very elongated orbits.

**Supplement:** the strength of the Sun's gravitational field decreases with distance from the Sun, so planets further out have lower orbital speeds (and much longer orbital periods, because their orbits are also longer).

**Supplement:** an object in an elliptical orbit travels fastest when it is closest to the Sun. As it moves inwards, it loses gravitational potential energy and gains the same amount of kinetic energy (energy is conserved), so it speeds up. Moving out again, kinetic energy is converted back to gravitational potential energy, and it slows down.

### The Sun as a star — 6.2.1

The Sun is a medium-sized star, made mostly of hydrogen and helium. It radiates most of its energy as infrared, visible light and ultraviolet.

**Supplement:** stars are powered by nuclear reactions that release energy. In stable stars, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium.

### Stars and galaxies — 6.2.2

- A galaxy is made up of many billions of stars.
- The Sun is a star in the galaxy called the Milky Way.
- The other stars in the Milky Way are much further from the Earth than the Sun is.
- Astronomical distances are measured in light-years. A light-year is the distance travelled by light in (the vacuum of) space in one year. It is a distance, not a time.

**Supplement:** one light-year is 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m.

**Supplement — the life cycle of a star:**

- A star forms from an interstellar cloud of gas and dust containing hydrogen (a nebula).
- Gravity pulls the cloud together; it collapses and heats up, becoming a protostar.
- When the centre is hot enough, hydrogen fuses into helium. The protostar becomes a stable star when the inward force of gravity is balanced by the outward force due to the high temperature (pressure) in its centre. The Sun is at this stage.
- Eventually every star runs out of hydrogen in its core.
- A star of about the Sun's mass expands to form a red giant. Its outer layers drift away as a planetary nebula, leaving a hot, small, dense white dwarf at the centre, which cools over a very long time.
- A much more massive star expands to form a red supergiant, which then explodes as a supernova. This forms a nebula containing hydrogen and new heavier elements, and leaves behind a neutron star or, for the most massive stars, a black hole.
- The nebula from a supernova may form new stars, with orbiting planets. The Sun and Earth contain heavy elements made in earlier stars.

### The Universe — 6.2.3

- The Milky Way is one of many billions of galaxies in the Universe. Its diameter is about 100 000 light-years.
- **Redshift:** an increase in the observed wavelength of electromagnetic radiation from a star or galaxy that is moving away (receding) from us. The light shifts towards the red end of the spectrum.
- Light from distant galaxies is redshifted compared with light from the same elements on Earth. The further away the galaxy, the greater the redshift, so the faster it is moving away.
- This shows that the Universe is expanding, which supports the Big Bang Theory: the Universe began from a single point about 14 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since.

**Supplement — cosmic microwave background radiation (CMBR):** microwave radiation of a specific frequency is detected coming from all directions in space. It was produced shortly after the Universe formed, as very high-energy (short-wavelength) radiation. As the Universe expanded, the radiation was stretched, so its wavelength increased into the microwave region. The CMBR is further evidence for the Big Bang.

**Supplement — the Hubble constant:**

- The speed v at which a galaxy moves away from us is found from the change in wavelength of its light (its redshift): a bigger change means a faster speed.
- The distance d to a far galaxy is found from the brightness of a supernova in it: supernovae of a certain type have a known peak brightness, so the dimmer one appears, the further away it is.
- Plotting v against d for many galaxies gives a straight line: v is proportional to d. The Hubble constant H₀ is the ratio v ÷ d. Its current estimate is 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ per second.
- d ÷ v = 1 ÷ H₀ is the time a galaxy has taken to reach its present distance at its present speed. It is the same for every galaxy, so it estimates the age of the Universe, and suggests all the matter in the Universe was once at a single point.

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: light travel time

**Example — Sun to Earth.** Distance 1.5 × 10¹¹ m. t = 1.5 × 10¹¹ ÷ (3.0 × 10⁸) = 500 s, about 8.3 minutes. We see the Sun as it was 8 minutes ago.

**Example — signal to Mars.** When Mars is 2.25 × 10¹¹ m away, a radio signal (also an EM wave) takes 2.25 × 10¹¹ ÷ (3.0 × 10⁸) = 750 s = 12.5 minutes. A reply takes another 12.5 minutes.

**Example — Moon.** The Moon is 3.84 × 10⁸ m away. t = 3.84 × 10⁸ ÷ (3.0 × 10⁸) = 1.28 s.

### Situation: light-years (Supplement)

**Example — nearest star.** Proxima Centauri is 4.2 light-years away. d = 4.2 × 9.5 × 10¹⁵ = 4.0 × 10¹⁶ m. Its light takes 4.2 years to reach us.

**Example — Milky Way.** 100 000 light-years = 1.0 × 10⁵ × 9.5 × 10¹⁵ = 9.5 × 10²⁰ m across.

### Situation: orbital speed (Supplement)

**Example — Earth.** r = 1.5 × 10¹¹ m, T = 365 days = 365 × 86 400 = 3.15 × 10⁷ s.

- v = 2π × 1.5 × 10¹¹ ÷ (3.15 × 10⁷) = 3.0 × 10⁴ m/s (30 km/s).

**Example — Moon.** r = 3.84 × 10⁸ m, T = 27.3 days = 2.36 × 10⁶ s. v = 2π × 3.84 × 10⁸ ÷ (2.36 × 10⁶) = 1.0 × 10³ m/s.

**Example — find the period.** A satellite orbits at r = 7.0 × 10⁶ m with speed 7.5 × 10³ m/s. T = 2πr ÷ v = 2π × 7.0 × 10⁶ ÷ (7.5 × 10³) = 5900 s, about 98 minutes.

**Example — compare planets.** Jupiter: r = 7.78 × 10¹¹ m, T = 11.9 years = 3.75 × 10⁸ s. v = 2π × 7.78 × 10¹¹ ÷ (3.75 × 10⁸) = 1.3 × 10⁴ m/s, less than half the Earth's speed: the further out, the slower.

### Situation: Hubble's law (Supplement)

**Example — find the speed.** A galaxy is 5.0 × 10²⁴ m away. v = H₀d = 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ × 5.0 × 10²⁴ = 1.1 × 10⁷ m/s.

**Example — find the distance.** A galaxy recedes at 6.6 × 10⁶ m/s. d = v ÷ H₀ = 6.6 × 10⁶ ÷ (2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸) = 3.0 × 10²⁴ m. In light-years: 3.0 × 10²⁴ ÷ (9.5 × 10¹⁵) = 3.2 × 10⁸ light-years.

**Example — age of the Universe.** 1 ÷ H₀ = 1 ÷ (2.2 × 10⁻¹⁸) = 4.5 × 10¹⁷ s. In years: 4.5 × 10¹⁷ ÷ (3.15 × 10⁷) = 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years, about 14 billion years.

### Situation: reading planetary data (Supplement)

**Example — which planet is gaseous?** From the table, Saturn's density (0.7 g/cm³) is far below the rocky planets' (about 4 to 5.5 g/cm³), so it is mostly gas.

**Example — weight on another planet.** A 60 kg astronaut on Mars (g = 3.7 N/kg) weighs 60 × 3.7 = 222 N, compared with 588 N on Earth.

## Exam checks

- A light-year is a distance.
- Day/night: rotation (24 h). Year and seasons: orbit (365 days) plus the tilted axis. Moon's phases: orbit (about a month).
- Convert days or years into seconds before using v = 2πr ÷ T; r is the radius, not the diameter.
- Inner planets: small, rocky, dense. Outer: large, gaseous. Explain with the accretion disc and temperature.
- Faster when closer to the Sun in an elliptical orbit: gravitational potential energy changes into kinetic energy.
- Star life cycle: nebula → protostar → stable star → red giant → planetary nebula + white dwarf (Sun-sized), or red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole (massive).
- Evidence for the Big Bang: redshift of distant galaxies (expanding Universe) and the CMBR.
