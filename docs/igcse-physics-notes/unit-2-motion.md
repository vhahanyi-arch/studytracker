# Unit 2 — Motion

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, section 1.2, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Speed and velocity — 1.2

Speed is the distance travelled per unit time. Velocity is speed in a given direction, so velocity is a vector and speed is a scalar. A car going round a roundabout at a steady 10 m/s has a constant speed but a changing velocity, because its direction changes.

Most journeys do not happen at a steady speed, so the useful quantity is the average speed: the total distance divided by the total time, including any time spent stopped.

### Distance–time graphs — 1.2

The gradient (steepness) of a distance–time graph is the speed.

- Horizontal line: at rest (distance not changing).
- Straight sloping line: constant speed. A steeper line means a faster speed.
- Curve getting steeper: accelerating.
- Curve getting less steep: decelerating.

To find a speed, pick two points far apart on a straight section, then divide the change in distance by the change in time.

### Speed–time graphs — 1.2

The gradient of a speed–time graph is the acceleration, and the area under it is the distance travelled.

- Horizontal line: constant speed (zero acceleration). On the time axis: at rest.
- Straight line sloping up: constant acceleration. Sloping down: constant deceleration.
- Curve (Supplement): changing acceleration. A curve that gets less steep shows acceleration decreasing, for example as air resistance grows.

For the distance, split the area into rectangles (constant speed) and triangles (constant acceleration from or to rest), or use trapezium areas, and add them up.

### Acceleration — 1.2 (Supplement)

Acceleration is the change in velocity per unit time. It is measured in m/s², meaning metres per second, every second. An acceleration of 3 m/s² means the velocity rises by 3 m/s each second.

A deceleration is a negative acceleration. If a car slows from 20 m/s to 8 m/s in 4 s, a = (8 − 20) ÷ 4 = −3 m/s², which is a deceleration of 3 m/s². Keep the minus sign in calculations and say "deceleration of 3 m/s²" in words.

### Free fall and terminal velocity — 1.2

Near the Earth's surface the acceleration of free fall, g, is approximately constant at about 9.8 m/s². Without air resistance, every object falls with this acceleration whatever its mass.

**Supplement:** with air (or liquid) resistance, a falling object behaves in three stages.

- At the start the speed is low, so air resistance is small; the resultant force is nearly the full weight and the object accelerates at about g.
- As the speed rises, air resistance increases, the resultant force (weight − air resistance) decreases, and the acceleration decreases. The speed–time graph curves and becomes less steep.
- When air resistance equals the weight, the resultant force is zero, the acceleration is zero and the object falls at a constant speed: its terminal velocity.

When a skydiver opens a parachute, air resistance suddenly becomes greater than the weight, so the resultant force is upward and the skydiver decelerates. As the speed falls, the drag falls, until it equals the weight again at a new, lower terminal velocity.

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| v = s ÷ t | v: speed, m/s; s: distance, m; t: time, s | Constant speed, or a single straight-line section of a distance–time graph. Rearranged: s = vt, t = s ÷ v. | Core |
| average speed = total distance ÷ total time | m/s | A whole journey with several stages, including stops. Never average the speeds of the stages. | Core |
| speed = gradient of distance–time graph | gradient = change in distance ÷ change in time | Reading speed from a graph. | Core |
| distance = area under speed–time graph | rectangle: base × height; triangle: ½ × base × height | Constant speed or constant acceleration sections. | Core |
| a = Δv ÷ Δt | a: acceleration, m/s²; Δv: change in velocity = final − initial, m/s; Δt: time taken, s | Any change in velocity. A negative answer is a deceleration. Rearranged: Δv = aΔt. | Supplement |
| acceleration = gradient of speed–time graph | gradient = change in speed ÷ change in time | Reading acceleration from a graph. | Supplement |

Unit conversion: to change km/h into m/s, divide by 3.6 (for example 72 km/h = 20 m/s). To change m/s into km/h, multiply by 3.6.

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: constant speed, find the missing quantity

**Example — time for a journey.** A train travels 45 km at a constant 25 m/s. How long does it take?

- Convert first: 45 km = 45 000 m.
- t = s ÷ v = 45 000 ÷ 25 = 1800 s = 30 minutes.

### Situation: a journey in stages

**Example — average speed.** A cyclist rides 6 km in 20 minutes, stops for 10 minutes, then rides 3 km in 15 minutes.

- Total distance = 6 + 3 = 9 km = 9000 m.
- Total time = 20 + 10 + 15 = 45 minutes = 2700 s.
- Average speed = 9000 ÷ 2700 = 3.3 m/s. The stop counts, because it is part of the total time.

### Situation: reading a distance–time graph

**Example — speed from the gradient.** A straight line on a distance–time graph goes from (2 s, 10 m) to (8 s, 40 m).

- Speed = (40 − 10) ÷ (8 − 2) = 30 ÷ 6 = 5.0 m/s.

### Situation: distance from a speed–time graph

**Example — area under the graph.** A car accelerates steadily from rest to 12 m/s in 6 s, then travels at 12 m/s for 10 s.

- Stage 1 (triangle): ½ × 6 × 12 = 36 m.
- Stage 2 (rectangle): 10 × 12 = 120 m.
- Total distance = 156 m.

**Example — trapezium.** A car slows steadily from 20 m/s to 10 m/s in 5 s. The area is a trapezium: ½ × (20 + 10) × 5 = 75 m. (You get the same answer by adding a 10 × 5 rectangle and a ½ × 5 × 10 triangle.)

### Situation: acceleration from changes in velocity (Supplement)

**Example — speeding up.** A bus goes from 4 m/s to 16 m/s in 8 s.

- a = (16 − 4) ÷ 8 = 1.5 m/s².

**Example — braking.** A car travelling at 30 m/s brakes and stops in 5 s.

- a = (0 − 30) ÷ 5 = −6 m/s², a deceleration of 6 m/s².

**Example — find the final speed.** A ball is dropped from rest and falls freely for 2.0 s (g = 9.8 m/s², no air resistance).

- Δv = aΔt = 9.8 × 2.0 = 19.6 m/s, so its speed is 19.6 m/s (from rest).

**Example — find the time.** A runner accelerates at 2.5 m/s² from 3 m/s to 8 m/s.

- Δt = Δv ÷ a = (8 − 3) ÷ 2.5 = 2.0 s.

### Situation: acceleration from a speed–time graph (Supplement)

**Example — gradient.** A straight line on a speed–time graph rises from 5 m/s at 0 s to 25 m/s at 4 s.

- a = (25 − 5) ÷ (4 − 0) = 5.0 m/s².
- If the line is curved, the acceleration is changing: a steeper part means a larger acceleration.

## Exam checks

- Check the units before substituting: km into m, minutes or hours into s.
- Distance–time graph: gradient = speed. Speed–time graph: gradient = acceleration, area = distance. Do not mix them up.
- Average speed uses total distance and total time, including stops.
- Δv is final minus initial. A negative acceleration is a deceleration.
- A curved speed–time line means a changing acceleration (Supplement); explain terminal velocity with forces: air resistance grows until it equals the weight, so the resultant force and acceleration become zero.
