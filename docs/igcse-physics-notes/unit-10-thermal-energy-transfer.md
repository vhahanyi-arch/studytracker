# Unit 10 — Transfer of thermal energy

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 2.3.1, 2.3.2, 2.3.3 and 2.3.4, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Conduction — 2.3.1

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through a material without the material itself moving. It is the main way energy passes through solids.

Good thermal conductors: metals (copper, aluminium, iron). Bad conductors (thermal insulators): plastic, wood, glass, air, wool, polystyrene.

**Experiments:** attach drawing pins with wax along rods of different materials of the same size, and heat one end of each equally (for example, rods arranged around the edge of a heated container). The pins drop off first from the best conductor. Or wrap paper around a rod that is half wood and half brass and pass it through a flame: the paper over the wood scorches, but the paper over the brass does not, because the brass conducts the energy away. Or heat the top of a test tube of water with ice weighted at the bottom: the water at the top boils while the ice stays frozen, showing water is a poor conductor.

**Supplement — how conduction works:** in all solids, the particles at the hot end vibrate more, and pass the vibration on to their neighbours through the bonds of the lattice (lattice vibration). In metals there is also a second, faster process: free (delocalised) electrons gain kinetic energy at the hot end, move quickly through the metal, and collide with particles further along, transferring energy. That is why metals are much better conductors than non-metals.

**Supplement:** in gases and most liquids the particles are far apart (or not held in a lattice), so collisions that pass on energy are much less frequent. That makes them bad conductors. Many solids (such as glass or brick) conduct better than insulators but much less well than metals.

### Convection — 2.3.2

Convection is the main way thermal energy moves through liquids and gases. It cannot happen in solids, because the particles cannot flow.

When part of a fluid is heated, it expands, so its density decreases and it rises. Cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place and is heated in turn. This sets up a convection current.

**Experiment:** drop a crystal of potassium permanganate into the bottom of a beaker of water near one edge, and heat gently below it. The purple colour rises above the heat, spreads across the top, and sinks down the far side, showing the convection current. For air, a candle under one chimney of a box draws smoke from a smouldering straw down the other chimney.

### Thermal radiation — 2.3.3

Thermal radiation is infrared radiation, part of the electromagnetic spectrum. All objects emit it; hotter objects emit more. It needs no medium, so it can travel through a vacuum, which is how energy reaches us from the Sun.

The surface affects radiation:

- Dull black surfaces are the best emitters and the best absorbers of infrared.
- Shiny white or silver surfaces are the worst emitters and worst absorbers; they are the best reflectors.

**Supplement:** an object stays at a constant temperature when it transfers energy away at the same rate as it receives energy. If it receives energy faster than it loses it, its temperature rises; if it loses energy faster, its temperature falls. The Earth's temperature depends on this balance between the incoming radiation from the Sun and the radiation the Earth's surface emits. Greenhouse gases (carbon dioxide, methane, water vapour) absorb and re-emit some of the infrared emitted by the surface, so less escapes to space and the average temperature rises; clouds, ice and snow reflect incoming radiation.

**Supplement:** the rate at which an object emits radiation increases with its surface temperature and with its surface area. A large-fin radiator or a large-eared desert fox loses energy faster.

**Supplement — emitters experiment (Leslie's cube):** a metal cube with four different faces (dull black, shiny black, dull white, shiny silver) is filled with hot water so all faces are at the same temperature. An infrared detector (or a thermometer with a blackened bulb) at the same distance from each face shows the dull black face emits most and the shiny silver face least.

**Supplement — absorbers experiment:** place two identical metal plates, one dull black and one shiny, at equal distances from a radiant heater, with a drawing pin stuck to the back of each with wax. The wax behind the dull black plate melts first, so its pin drops first. Or use two thermometers, one with a blackened bulb, in sunlight.

### Consequences of thermal energy transfer — 2.3.4

- **Kitchen pan:** the base is metal, a good conductor, so energy passes quickly from the hob to the food. The handle is plastic or wood, an insulator, so it stays cool enough to hold.
- **Heating a room:** a "radiator" works mainly by convection. Air next to it is heated, expands, becomes less dense and rises; cooler air sinks to replace it, circulating warm air around the room. Heaters are placed low down; a cooling unit in a fridge is placed at the top.
- **Vacuum flask:** the vacuum stops conduction and convection; silvered walls reduce radiation; the stopper reduces convection and evaporation.
- **Insulating a house:** loft insulation and cavity-wall foam trap air in small pockets, so it cannot move (no convection) and air is a poor conductor; double glazing has an air or vacuum gap.

**Supplement — more than one process at once:**

- **A fire burning wood or coal:** radiation heats people and objects in front of it (it travels in straight lines through air); convection carries hot gases and smoke up the chimney while fresh air is drawn in at the bottom, feeding the fire; conduction heats the grate and surrounding brickwork.
- **A car radiator:** the hot engine heats the coolant by conduction; the coolant is pumped to the radiator, which has thin metal fins with a large surface area; energy is conducted through the metal, then carried away by air flowing past (convection, helped by the fan and the car's motion), and some is radiated.

## Using the ideas in different situations

### Situation: choose a material

**Example — saucepan.** Copper base: a good conductor, so energy reaches the food quickly. Plastic handle: a poor conductor, so it stays cool.

**Example — winter clothing.** Several thin layers trap air, a poor conductor; the trapped air cannot move, so convection is reduced.

### Situation: explain with convection

**Example — sea breeze.** By day the land heats up faster than the sea. Air over the land is heated, expands, becomes less dense and rises; cooler air from over the sea moves in to replace it.

**Example — where to put a heater.** At the bottom of a room, so the warm, less dense air rises and circulates. A freezer compartment goes at the top of a fridge, so cooled, denser air sinks.

### Situation: explain with radiation

**Example — solar water panel.** The pipes are painted dull black so they absorb as much infrared as possible.

**Example — teapot or survival blanket.** A shiny surface emits little infrared, so the contents or the person stay warm longer.

**Example — houses in hot countries** are painted white to reflect infrared, so they absorb less and stay cooler.

### Situation: energy balance (Supplement)

**Example — car parked in the sun.** In the morning, the car receives more energy from the Sun per second than it emits, so it warms up. As it gets hotter, it emits more radiation per second, until emission equals absorption and its temperature stops rising.

**Example — Earth.** If greenhouse gases increase, less infrared escapes per second than the Earth receives, so the Earth warms until it emits enough to balance again, at a higher temperature.

## Exam checks

- Conduction: solids (lattice vibrations; free electrons in metals). Convection: liquids and gases only. Radiation: needs no medium.
- Explain convection with expand → less dense → rises; never say "heat rises".
- Dull black is best at both emitting and absorbing. Shiny is worst at both, best at reflecting.
- Thermal radiation is infrared, an electromagnetic wave.
- Trapped air insulates because it is a poor conductor and it cannot move to form convection currents.
- A constant temperature means the rates of energy in and out are equal, not that no energy is moving.
