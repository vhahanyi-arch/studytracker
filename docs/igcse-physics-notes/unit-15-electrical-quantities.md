# Unit 15 — Electrical quantities

Source: Cambridge IGCSE Physics 0625 syllabus, sections 4.2.1, 4.2.2, 4.2.3, 4.2.4 and 4.2.5, Core and Supplement. See [source and scope](README.md).

## Concept summary

### Electric charge — 4.2.1

There are two kinds of charge, positive and negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.

Rubbing two insulators together (a polythene rod with a cloth) transfers electrons from one to the other. Only negative charges (electrons) move. The object that gains electrons becomes negatively charged; the one that loses them is left positively charged, with equal and opposite charge. Polythene gains electrons (negative); acetate loses electrons (positive).

**Experiments:** rub a rod and hang it from a thread; bring another charged rod near: it is repelled (same charge) or attracted (opposite charge). Detect charge with a gold-leaf electroscope (the leaf rises when the cap is charged) or by the rod picking up small pieces of paper.

**Conductors and insulators:** metals have free electrons that can move through them, so charge flows: they are conductors (copper, aluminium, graphite). In insulators the electrons are held tightly in place and cannot flow (plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood). To test, put the material in a gap in a circuit with a cell and a lamp: if the lamp lights, it is a conductor.

**Supplement:** charge is measured in coulombs (C). An electric field is a region in which an electric charge experiences a force. The direction of the field at a point is the direction of the force on a positive charge there.

**Supplement — field patterns:**

- Around a positive point charge: straight lines radiating outwards. Negative point charge: straight lines pointing inwards.
- Around a charged conducting sphere: the same radial pattern outside the sphere, at 90° to its surface.
- Between two oppositely charged parallel plates: evenly spaced parallel lines from the positive plate to the negative plate (a uniform field), ignoring the edges.

### Electric current — 4.2.2

Electric current is a flow of charge. In metals the charge carriers are free electrons. Current is measured in amperes (A) with an ammeter connected in series. Analogue ammeters have a needle and scale; digital ammeters show a number. Choose a range just above the expected current for the most precise reading (for example a 0 to 1 A range for 0.35 A rather than 0 to 10 A).

Direct current (d.c.) flows in one direction only (from a cell or battery). Alternating current (a.c.) keeps reversing direction (the mains supply, 50 Hz or 60 Hz depending on the country).

**Supplement:** current is the charge passing a point per unit time. Conventional current flows from positive to negative around a circuit; the free electrons actually flow the other way, from negative to positive.

### E.m.f. and potential difference — 4.2.3

**Electromotive force (e.m.f.):** the electrical work done by a source in moving a unit charge around a complete circuit. Measured in volts (V). A 6 V battery gives 6 J to each coulomb of charge.

**Potential difference (p.d.):** the work done by a unit charge passing through a component, which is the energy transferred per unit charge from electrical to other forms in that component. Measured in volts with a voltmeter connected in parallel across the component.

E.m.f. is energy given to the charge by the source; p.d. is energy given up by the charge in a component. As with ammeters, choose the voltmeter range just above the expected reading.

### Resistance — 4.2.4

Resistance opposes the current. R = V ÷ I; unit ohm (Ω). A larger resistance needs a larger p.d. to drive the same current.

**Experiment:** connect the component in series with an ammeter, a variable resistor and a supply; connect a voltmeter in parallel across the component. Adjust the variable resistor to get several pairs of V and I readings; calculate R = V ÷ I for each, or plot I against V.

For a metal wire: a longer wire has a larger resistance; a thicker wire (larger cross-sectional area) has a smaller resistance. **Supplement:** resistance is directly proportional to length and inversely proportional to cross-sectional area. Double the length → double the resistance. Double the area → half the resistance.

**Supplement — current–voltage (I–V) graphs:**

- **Fixed resistor (at constant temperature):** a straight line through the origin. Current is proportional to p.d.; the resistance is constant.
- **Filament lamp:** a curve that gets less steep as V increases. As the current increases, the filament gets hotter, its resistance increases, and the current rises less for each extra volt. The graph is the same shape for reversed p.d.
- **Diode:** almost no current in the reverse direction (very high resistance); in the forward direction, current flows once the p.d. passes a small threshold (about 0.6 V), then rises steeply (low resistance). A diode lets current flow one way only.

### Electrical energy and power — 4.2.5

Circuits transfer energy from a source (a cell, battery or mains supply) to the components, and from them to the surroundings (light, heating, kinetic energy of a motor).

Power P = IV. Energy E = IVt (= Pt).

**The kilowatt-hour (kWh):** the energy transferred by a 1 kW appliance in 1 hour. 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3 600 000 J. Electricity companies charge per kWh ("unit").

## Key formulas

| Formula | Symbols and units | When to use it | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| R = V ÷ I | R: resistance, Ω; V: p.d., V; I: current, A | Any component. Rearranged: V = IR, I = V ÷ R. | Core |
| P = IV | P: power, W; I: A; V: V | Power of an appliance or component. | Core |
| E = IVt | E: energy, J; t: time, s | Energy transferred in a time. Equivalent: E = Pt. | Core |
| energy in kWh = power in kW × time in hours | kWh | Electricity bills. | Core |
| cost = energy in kWh × price per kWh | | Cost of using an appliance. | Core |
| I = Q ÷ t | I: current, A; Q: charge, C; t: time, s | Charge flowing in a time. Rearranged: Q = It. | Supplement |
| E = W ÷ Q | E: e.m.f., V; W: work done by the source, J; Q: charge, C | Energy given to charge by a source. | Supplement |
| V = W ÷ Q | V: p.d., V; W: energy transferred in the component, J | Energy given up by charge in a component. Rearranged: W = QV. | Supplement |
| R ∝ L; R ∝ 1 ÷ A | L: length, m; A: cross-sectional area, m² | Comparing wires of the same metal. | Supplement |

## Using the formulas in different situations

### Situation: resistance from meter readings

**Example — resistor.** A voltmeter reads 6.0 V and an ammeter reads 0.30 A. R = 6.0 ÷ 0.30 = 20 Ω.

**Example — find the current.** A 12 V supply across a 48 Ω heater. I = 12 ÷ 48 = 0.25 A.

**Example — find the p.d.** 0.50 A flows through a 220 Ω resistor. V = IR = 0.50 × 220 = 110 V.

**Example — mA.** A p.d. of 3.0 V drives 15 mA through an LED circuit resistor. I = 0.015 A, R = 3.0 ÷ 0.015 = 200 Ω.

### Situation: charge and current (Supplement)

**Example — charge.** A current of 2.0 A flows for 3 minutes. Q = It = 2.0 × 180 = 360 C.

**Example — time.** How long for 50 C to pass with a current of 0.25 A? t = Q ÷ I = 50 ÷ 0.25 = 200 s.

### Situation: energy per coulomb (Supplement)

**Example — e.m.f.** A battery does 540 J of work moving 45 C round a circuit. E = 540 ÷ 45 = 12 V.

**Example — p.d.** 30 C passes through a lamp with a p.d. of 6.0 V across it. Energy transferred W = QV = 30 × 6.0 = 180 J.

### Situation: power

**Example — kettle.** A kettle takes 10 A from a 230 V supply. P = 10 × 230 = 2300 W = 2.3 kW.

**Example — find the current.** A 60 W lamp on 230 V: I = P ÷ V = 60 ÷ 230 = 0.26 A.

### Situation: energy in joules

**Example — heater.** A 12 V, 2.0 A heater runs for 5 minutes. E = IVt = 2.0 × 12 × 300 = 7200 J.

### Situation: cost of electricity

**Example — one appliance.** A 2.5 kW heater is used for 4 hours a day for 30 days, at 15 cents per kWh.

- Energy = 2.5 × 4 × 30 = 300 kWh.
- Cost = 300 × 15 = 4500 cents = 45.00 dollars.

**Example — watts and minutes.** A 800 W microwave runs for 45 minutes. Energy = 0.8 kW × 0.75 h = 0.6 kWh. At 20 cents per kWh, cost = 12 cents.

**Example — kWh to joules.** 0.6 kWh = 0.6 × 3 600 000 = 2 160 000 J.

### Situation: wire dimensions (Supplement)

**Example — longer and thicker.** A wire has resistance 8.0 Ω. A wire of the same metal, twice as long and with twice the cross-sectional area: × 2 for length, ÷ 2 for area, so still 8.0 Ω.

**Example — half the diameter.** Halving the diameter makes the area a quarter (area ∝ diameter²), so the resistance becomes 4 times larger: 32 Ω.

### Situation: reading I–V graphs (Supplement)

**Example — lamp.** From an I–V graph a lamp carries 0.20 A at 2.0 V and 0.30 A at 6.0 V. R = 2.0 ÷ 0.20 = 10 Ω at first and 6.0 ÷ 0.30 = 20 Ω later: the resistance increases as the filament heats up.

## Exam checks

- Ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel.
- Convert mA to A (÷ 1000), kΩ to Ω (× 1000), minutes to s (× 60).
- For cost, use kW and hours; for joules, use W and seconds.
- Charging by friction moves electrons only.
- Conventional current: + to −. Electron flow: − to +.
- Filament lamp: resistance increases with temperature. Diode: current one way only.
- In R ∝ 1 ÷ A, the area depends on the diameter squared.
